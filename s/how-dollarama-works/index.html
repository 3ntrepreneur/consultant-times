How Dollarama works

Confirmed · 12 min read

Open in the full site →

The short version

Dollarama sells everyday goods, seasonal items and consumables at a fixed set of low prices, and it has turned that simple idea into one of the most profitable retail formats in Canada. In the year ended February 1, 2026, it reported sales of C$7,255.8 million, EBITDA of C$2,408.2 million (a 33.2% margin) and net earnings of C$1,309.4 million, which is about 18.0% of sales (computed). Most grocers and general merchants dream of an operating margin in the single digits. Dollarama reported 26.7%.

The master question is where the profit comes from. The answer is not one product. It is a loop: buy a large share of the range directly from factories overseas, price it at a few round numbers, put it in small, cheap-to-run stores, and let frequent low-ticket visits fill those stores. Cheap goods bring traffic, traffic fills the stores, full stores pay back quickly, and quick payback funds the next store.

Under the 60-second test, the customer pays at the till, once per visit, for a basket of low-priced goods. Revenue is repeat rather than contractual: there is no subscription and no consumable lock-in, so loyalty comes from habit and price. Dollarama owns its distribution network and buys its goods, so it is asset-heavy in logistics but mostly leases its stores. What would break it is covered at the end.

How it makes money

There are three engines. The first and largest is Canada, with 1,734 stores at the end of the second quarter of fiscal 2027 (13 weeks ended August 2, 2026). The second is Dollarcity, a Latin American dollar-store chain in which Dollarama holds equity-accounted stakes, so it contributes earnings but not sales. The third is Australia, where Dollarama bought The Reject Shop in July 2025 and now runs 414 stores. Consolidated second-quarter sales were C$2,026.6 million, up 17.6%, helped by the Australian addition.

Canadian pricing is a ladder. According to the 2026 Annual Information Form, items range from C$0.25 up to C$5.00. Dollarcity's range is US$0.69 to US$4.00, and Australia's is A$0.45 to A$15.00. The ladder matters because it lets management trade up the basket, for example by selling a C$4 or C$5 item that would once have sat in a department store, without losing the identity of a low-price shop. The Form says seasonal products were about 13% of Canadian sales in fiscal 2026, which gives a steady flow of reasons to visit.

The mix is also protective. The Form states that 65% of Canadian sales came from private label and 35% from national brands. Private label carries a better margin and cannot be price-compared in a rival store, because the same item does not exist there.

Dollarcity is the quiet profit engine. Dollarama's share of Dollarcity's net earnings for calendar 2025 was C$191.5 million, up 47.4%. That equals about 14.6% of Dollarama's fiscal 2026 net earnings (computed, though the periods differ by a month). Dollarcity had 781 stores on June 30, 2026, against 658 a year earlier, and its sales grew 30.0% in the second quarter. Dollarama holds 60.1% of the main operating company (CARS) and 80.05% of ICM, the entity expanding in Mexico.

The economics

Start with the income statement for fiscal 2026: gross margin 45.0%, selling, general and administrative (SG&A) expense 15.1% of sales, EBITDA margin 33.2% and operating margin 26.7%. Capital spending was only C$272.8 million, about 3.8% of sales (computed). A business that earns a 33% EBITDA margin and spends under 4% of sales on capex converts a large share of earnings to cash. That is the profile of a very good retailer.

The source of the gross margin is direct import. The Form says 46% of Canadian procurement volume in fiscal 2026 was overseas direct imports, from more than 25 countries, with the vast majority from China. The other 54% came from North American vendors. Buying direct removes the wholesaler's margin and lets Dollarama specify the product and the pack size. Concentration risk is modest: the largest vendor is about 6.2% of Canadian purchases and the top ten about 35%.

The store economics explain the growth plan. Per the Form, a new Canadian store needs about C$1 million of capital and inventory, net of tenant allowance, and pays back in about two years. New stores are expected to sell more than C$3.2 million in their first two years, and the average store sells about C$4.1 million. On an average footprint of 10,455 square feet, that is roughly C$392 per square foot (computed). Management targets about 2,200 Canadian stores by 2034, which is about 466 more than today's 1,734 (computed). Guidance for fiscal 2027 calls for 65 to 75 net new Canadian stores.

The margin did slip in the most recent quarter, and the cause is mix rather than the Canadian engine. Second-quarter consolidated gross margin was 44.5% against 45.5% a year earlier, and SG&A rose to 15.1% from 14.0%, which pushed EBITDA margin down to 32.2% from 34.1%. Canada alone had a 45.7% gross margin and a 34.9% EBITDA margin. Australia reported a 32.4% gross margin, SG&A at 28.1% of sales, EBITDA of C$10.2 million on C$184.8 million of sales (about 5.5%, computed) and an operating loss of C$17.1 million. Australia reduced diluted earnings per share by C$0.05 in the quarter, and management still expects a net loss there for fiscal 2027.

The Australian purchase shows the trade-off. The Reject Shop was bought for A$233.6 million, from an offer of A$6.68 a share, according to the Form. It brings a second large market and a store base, but at a fraction of Canadian profitability for now. Dollarama is refitting stores, with 60 using its layout and fixtures, while keeping the old banner until the range better reflects its value offer.

The number that runs the business

The number is comparable store sales in Canada, because it tells you whether the existing store base is getting more productive. Fiscal 2026 comparable sales rose 4.2%, and the second quarter of fiscal 2027 rose 5.4%, made up of 3.7% more transactions and a 1.7% larger average basket. Growth in transactions is the healthier half: it means more people are visiting, not just paying more per visit. In September 2026, management raised its fiscal 2027 comparable sales guidance to 4.0% to 4.5% from 3.0% to 4.0%.

Why does this one figure matter so much? Because the cost base is largely fixed once a store opens, in the form of rent, staff and lighting. Extra sales on the same space flow to profit at a high rate. It also validates the payback maths: a store that grows its sales after the two-year ramp is a better investment than the initial model assumed.

Watch two companions. The first is gross margin in Canada, guided at 45.0% to 45.5% for fiscal 2027, because that shows whether imported costs, freight and currency are being passed through. The second is net new stores, because comparable sales exclude them and the growth story needs both. A company opening 65 to 75 stores a year on a base of 1,734 is adding about 4% to its store count (computed).

Where AI and automation hit

Retail automation lands in places where Dollarama is already structurally strong. Demand forecasting and replenishment matter for a chain with thousands of low-priced items and a large seasonal range, because an overstocked item at a low price point has little room for markdown. Assortment and pricing tools can help management decide which products justify a C$4 or C$5 slot on the ladder.

Logistics is the other area. Dollarama says it operates seven warehouses and one distribution centre in Canada, with a Western Canada hub near Calgary due by the end of calendar 2027. That project is the main reason capital spending is guided at C$420 million to C$470 million for fiscal 2027, against C$272.8 million in fiscal 2026. Automated sorting and routing are typical in such facilities, though the company's filings that I reviewed do not quantify any automation savings, so this is a reasoned expectation, not a reported result.

The honest view is that AI is a modest lever here. The costs that matter most are the goods and the store labour, and the customer's trip to a physical shop cannot be replaced by software. Online shopping is not a significant disclosed line in the figures reviewed, which suggests the store remains the engine. The more relevant technology threat is indirect: online marketplaces selling similar imported goods at low prices, which is a pricing risk rather than an automation one.

Who wins and why

Dollarama wins on cost and scale, not on network effects or switching costs. The moat checklist is clear. Scale and cost advantage are the real ones: direct sourcing from overseas factories, a distribution network that serves more than 1,700 Canadian stores, and a store model that pays back in about two years. A rival with fewer stores buys in smaller volumes and carries higher unit costs. Brand is a secondary moat, since the name stands for low prices to Canadian shoppers. Switching costs are close to zero, so the moat depends on staying cheaper and more convenient than the alternatives.

The competition looks fragmented and small next to it. The Form names Dollar Tree Canada, Your Dollar Store With More, Great Canadian Dollar Store and Buck or Two Plus! as pure-play dollar chains, with about 500 stores between them (Dollarama's own estimate). Dollarama's 1,734 Canadian stores are more than three times that figure (computed). The larger threat is from general merchants and grocers such as Walmart, which can discount many of the same categories as part of a bigger trip. That comparison is my own judgement, not a figure from the filings, but it explains why Dollarama keeps adding consumables and keeps its prices low: it competes for the everyday basket, not just the occasional impulse item.

Capital allocation is disciplined and returns cash. In fiscal 2026 the company repurchased 4,426,267 shares for C$834.2 million at an average of C$188.47. That equals about 63.7% of fiscal 2026 net earnings (computed). In the second quarter of fiscal 2027 it bought another 1,596,016 shares for C$300.4 million. The current normal course issuer bid allows up to 13,532,086 shares, or 5.0% of those outstanding. The quarterly dividend was raised 13.4% to C$0.12 a share. Net debt was C$2,517.0 million at the end of the second quarter, or 2.12 times adjusted EBITDA, which is moderate and sits within a business that funds growth and buybacks from cash flow, with new notes issued in 2026 to refinance.

What would break it? Five things deserve attention. First, tariffs, freight and currency changes, because 46% of Canadian purchase volume is imported and most of it from China. Second, comparable sales turning negative, which would expose the fixed cost base. Third, a saturated Canadian market, since the 2,200-store target implies only about 466 more locations. Fourth, Australia consuming management time and capital without reaching Canadian returns, with a net loss still expected for fiscal 2027. Fifth, valuation: the company buys back shares at prices near C$188, so poor returns on that spending would be a self-inflicted loss.

In a case interview you could say: Dollarama is a low-price, high-turnover retailer that earns a 33% EBITDA margin because it imports roughly half of what it sells, runs cheap stores that pay back in about two years, and funds expansion and buybacks from cash flow, so it stays ahead as long as it keeps costs below those of rivals and customers keep coming back.

What it means: Dollarama is a low-price, high-turnover retailer whose margin comes from direct import, private label and cheap stores that pay back in about two years. The winners in this format are the chains with the scale to buy cheaper than rivals and the store economics to keep opening more.
