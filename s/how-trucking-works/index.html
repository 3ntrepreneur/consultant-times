How trucking makes money (and why it barely does)

Confirmed · 9 min read

Open in the full site →

The short version

Trucking moves most of the goods in North America, and it is a business of very small margins and very large numbers of players. Shippers (the manufacturers and retailers with goods to move) pay carriers (the companies that own the trucks) by the mile or by the load. Brokers sit between them, matching loads to trucks and keeping a spread.

The basic problem is that the costs are mostly fixed or hard to control, while the price is set by supply and demand in an open market. When there are more trucks than freight, rates fall below what it costs to run them, and carriers keep driving anyway because a parked truck still has a payment due.

How it makes money

A carrier earns revenue per loaded mile, under either a contract with a shipper (a rate agreed for a year or so) or on the spot market (a price for a single load, set daily). Contract rates give stability; spot rates swing with demand and tend to run below costs when the market is weak.

The numbers show how tight things have been. FreightWaves, drawing on ATRI and its own SONAR rate data, reports that contract linehaul rates once cleared carriers' non-fuel costs by about $1.01 per loaded mile in 2022. By 2025 that cushion had shrunk to $0.26. Spot rates sat below non-fuel cost every year from 2023 to 2025. Dry van contract rates averaged $2.69 a mile in the third quarter of 2026, which FreightWaves says is below fourth-quarter 2021 levels, while non-fuel costs are about 30% higher than in 2021. In its view, the 2026 rate rise is a correction rather than a boom.

The economics

The industry benchmark is the American Transportation Research Institute's annual survey. Its 2026 edition, covering 2025, put the average cost of operating a truck at $2.336 per mile, up 3.4% and the highest in the report's history. Excluding fuel, costs rose 4.2% to $1.854 per mile.

The main lines: driver wages were about 82 cents per mile, with another 21 cents for benefits. Fuel was about 48 cents. Repair and maintenance rose 8.6% to 21.5 cents. Insurance was 10.6 cents per mile, up 3.9% in a year and 23.9% since 2021. Tariffs pushed tyre costs up 6.4% to about 5 cents. According to ATRI, only fuel and driver pay rose more slowly than inflation.

ATRI's previous report, covering 2024, found operating margins had fallen below 2%. Reading across both reports: a carrier that mismanages a few cents per mile can move from profit to loss.

The number that runs the business

The number is the share of miles driven empty. In ATRI's report on 2024 data, empty miles averaged 16.7%. Every empty mile still burns fuel, driver time and tyres but earns nothing.

A simple illustration: if a truck costs $2.34 per mile driven and 16.7% of miles are empty, the cost of each loaded mile is about $2.80 (2.336 divided by 0.833). Pricing a load at $2.50 a mile can therefore lose money even when it looks profitable. This is why carriers fight for backhauls, loads on the return leg, and why dense networks and repeat lanes beat one-off spot freight.

Where AI and automation hit

The most developed uses are digital freight matching (software that finds a load for an empty truck), route and fuel optimisation, and automated brokerage that prices and books freight without a phone call. Each of these targets the same issues: empty miles and brokers' spreads.

Autonomous trucks are a longer-term story and are not yet a material part of the economics. The nearer-term win for most fleets is data: knowing their true cost per mile by lane and by customer.

Who wins and why

Winners are carriers with dense, repeat freight and low empty miles, those with strong contracts instead of spot exposure, and brokers with better pricing data. Losers are small fleets that cannot absorb a record insurance and maintenance bill.

Industry voices quoted by trade press describe 2026 as a turning point for freight rates, but also say costs are rising fast enough that fleets must keep cutting costs. For consultants, trucking is a clean example of a fragmented industry in which operational detail, not strategy slides, decides who survives.
