How Aritzia works

Confirmed · 9 min read

Open in the full site →

The short version

Aritzia is a Vancouver-based women's fashion brand that designs nearly everything it sells, has it made by outside factories, and sells it through its own boutiques and its own website and app. About 97% of net revenue comes from its exclusive brands, such as Aritzia, Wilfred, Babaton, TNA and The Super Puff, according to its annual information form for the year ended March 1, 2026. It sits at what it calls an attainable price point, between fast fashion and luxury. All dollar figures below are Canadian unless stated.

The master question for this piece is where the profit actually comes from. The answer is not clothing in general. It is full-price sales of a tightly edited, in-house range, sold through a small number of large, well-placed stores that also pull customers online. If the boutiques stopped selling, much of the profit would go with them, because the company says about half of boutique transactions are preceded by a digital interaction and opening a store in a new market lifts online sales there by about 65% on average.

The latest numbers show a machine running hot. In the 13 weeks ended August 30, 2026 (the second quarter of fiscal 2027), net revenue rose 44.1% to $1.17 billion, and comparable sales rose 34.5%. The United States was 66.6% of revenue, up 60.3%, while Canada grew 19.8%. The growth story is now mostly an American one.

How it makes money

Run the 60-second business-model test. Who pays, and for what? The customer pays, directly, for apparel and accessories. There is no advertiser, no marketplace take rate and no franchise royalty. Aritzia is a vertically integrated brand: the AIF says it controls design, merchandise planning, sourcing, production and retail for its exclusive brands. The only third-party product in the mix is a complement of premium denim, accessories and footwear.

Is revenue one-off or recurring? One-off, per purchase, so the real question is repeat behaviour rather than churn. The AIF says loyal clients who shop across channels spend three times more than single-channel clients. At the October 2026 investor day, management said digitally acquired customers are twice as likely to shop in both channels. That is the closest thing to a recurring engine here: a habit rather than a contract.

Is there a locked-in consumable? No. A coat or a pair of trousers can be bought elsewhere tomorrow, which is why the model leans on brand and scarcity instead. Who owns the assets? Mostly Aritzia, and that makes it asset-heavier than a typical brand. It runs company-operated distribution centres (Vaughan, Ontario at 550,000 sq ft, plus New Westminster, British Columbia), uses a third-party operator for Columbus, Ohio (560,000 sq ft), and is building a 380,000 sq ft site in Delta, British Columbia with planned robotics. It leases its boutiques, which average about 10,000 sq ft, and pays for the fit-out. Factories are outsourced: finished goods come from manufacturers in 15 countries, and the company does not hold long-term contracts with them.

The revenue engines, named plainly, are three. First, boutique retail: $766.9 million in Q2, up 34.1% and 65.6% of revenue. Second, digital, meaning the website and app: $402.9 million, up 67.7% and 34.4% of revenue. Third, a small tail of other brands and formats, including Reigning Champ men's wear (four boutiques, which Aritzia counts separately), plus a small food and beverage team. Over fiscal 2021 to 2026, ecommerce grew at a 25% compound annual rate, per the AIF.

What would break it? Four things: a tariff or duty shock on imported goods, a weak Canadian dollar against the US dollar, a fashion miss that leaves unsold inventory and forces markdowns, and a brand that loses its edge as it doubles in size. The AIF lists the first three as named risks. The fourth is the one a consultant should worry about most.

The economics

Start with the margin structure for the quarter. Reported gross profit was $667.4 million, a 57.1% margin, but that includes $97.4 million of refunds on tariffs paid earlier. Stripping those out, adjusted gross profit was $570.0 million, a 48.7% margin, up 490 basis points on a year earlier. Our arithmetic (computed): the refunds account for roughly 8.4 points of the reported margin, and about 15% of reported gross profit. Anyone quoting the 57.1% figure as the underlying run rate is wrong.

Below gross profit, selling, general and administrative costs were $345.4 million, 29.5% of revenue, compared with 30.8% a year earlier. Dollars rose 38.1%, but revenue rose faster, so the cost base was spread more thinly. That operating leverage is the heart of the profit story. Adjusted EBITDA reached $246.2 million, a 21.0% margin versus 15.2%. Net income was $201.7 million, but adjusted net income, which removes the refund effect and other items, was $156.0 million (computed difference: about $45.7 million). Diluted EPS was $1.70 as reported and $1.31 adjusted.

Why do margins rise when sales rise this quickly? Much of the cost is fixed per boutique, such as rent, a store manager and the fit-out, so extra sales fall mostly to profit. Fewer markdowns would also lift gross margin, though the filings reviewed give no markdown figure.

New stores carry the growth, and the claimed payback is unusually fast. At the investor day, management said new boutiques pay back in under 12 months, against a 12 to 18 month planning assumption. That is a company statement made in a presentation, so treat it as a claim to test rather than an audited fact. Over the past four years boutique count grew about 7% a year while square footage grew about 14% a year, which means the company is building bigger boutiques, not simply more of them. A planned four-level flagship of roughly 41,800 sq ft at Vancouver's CF Pacific Centre shows the direction.

The balance sheet is sound for a business that is growing this fast. Cash was $528.1 million at quarter end versus $352.3 million a year earlier. Inventory was $714.9 million, up 35.8%, which is slower than revenue growth of 44.1% (computed comparison). Capital spending was $62.4 million in the quarter, and the company guides to about $250 million for fiscal 2027.

Guidance frames the year. For fiscal 2027, revenue is expected at $4.78 billion to $4.88 billion, up roughly 29% to 32%, with an adjusted EBITDA margin of about 20.0% (17.8% in fiscal 2026). The plan assumes 12 to 13 new boutiques plus four to five repositions, with 11 to 12 of the new ones in the US. Q3 revenue is guided to $1.275 billion to $1.325 billion, up about 23% to 27%, which is a notable slowdown from Q2 and a reminder that the comparison base is getting harder.

The number that runs the business

Many retailers watch same-store sales. For Aritzia, the number that explains the model is sales per unit of space, backed by how quickly a new boutique earns back its cost. The company does not publish sales per square foot in the documents we reviewed, so we cannot give that figure. What it does publish is the payback claim above, the 7% versus 14% growth gap between store count and square footage, and the Q2 comparable sales growth of 34.5%.

Here is why that matters. A boutique is a big fixed bet: long lease, fit-out, staff, and inventory. Its profit depends on how much merchandise moves through a square foot at full price. Management's plan shows it believes in bigger formats: it wants 5 to 7 repositions a year, where an old small store is replaced by a larger one in a better location, and it is shifting capital accordingly. Of roughly $2 billion of planned five-year capital spending, about $1.1 billion is for retail square footage, about $500 million for US distribution and about $400 million for technology, digital, offices and maintenance.

The strategic bet is that productivity per square foot can hold as the network expands from 146 boutiques today to something much larger. The fiscal 2032 target is $9.5 billion to $10.5 billion of revenue, a 15% to 17% compound growth rate, with an adjusted EBITDA margin of about 21%. Management expects US boutiques to reach 160 by fiscal 2032 from 87 at the end of fiscal 2027, and assumes comparable sales growth only in the low to mid single digits. In other words, today's 30-plus percent comps are not the plan; the plan relies on new space. If new stores open at lower productivity than the early winners, the whole arithmetic weakens. Management also expects the US to supply just over 85% of incremental growth, with Canada at about 5%.

Canada is being treated as mature. Management said the Canadian boutique count will decline slightly as legacy Wilfred, TNA and Babaton stores are folded into larger Aritzia boutiques, such as the Toronto Eaton Centre flagship opened in April 2026. That is a good example of how the metric guides decisions: fewer, bigger, better-placed stores.

Where AI and automation hit

Aritzia has said little in public about AI, and honesty requires saying so. The Q2 release and the five-year plan coverage we reviewed did not discuss AI. The AIF does carry a risk factor on AI and refers to reliance on third-party models. We make no claim about any payoff.

Where automation is visible, it is in the physical supply chain and inventory. First, Aritzia announced in December 2025 that it would deploy Nedap's RFID-based ID Cloud platform to get item-level stock accuracy, aimed at availability in stores and online and at ship-from-store, in-store pickup and returns. The coverage reported no measured results for Aritzia itself, so the benefits are expected, not proven. Second, the new Delta, British Columbia distribution centre is planned with robotics and increased automation, and the Ohio facility was slated for a retrofit in fiscal 2027.

Why does this matter for the economics? Digital revenue is growing at 67.7% and is a third of sales, and online orders cost more to pick, pack and return than boutique sales. Ship-from-store and buy-online-pick-up-in-store, which the AIF says most boutiques offer, only work if the stock count is right. RFID reduces the chance of promising an item that is not there. Better inventory visibility also supports the thing that drives margin: selling at full price with less excess stock to clear.

For a consultant, the useful question is where automation earns a return. In the warehouse, it pays when volumes are high and predictable, which is what the American push now promises: the plan puts about $500 million into US distribution. In design and the stores, the case is far less obvious, because the brand's value is a point of view and service. Automation here is a cost lever, not a moat.

Who wins and why

Check the moat list. Brand is the main one: Aritzia has built a name that lets it hold prices well above fast fashion, and its exclusive-brand share of about 97% means no one else sells its product. Scale is a second, growing source of advantage, because bigger volumes spread design, distribution and marketing costs. Cost advantage is thin: it outsources production to many countries with no long-term contracts, so it has flexibility rather than a cost edge. There are no network effects, switching costs or regulation. Vertical integration is used where differentiation lives, in design and merchandise planning, and outsourced where it is commodity, in sewing.

The tariff episode shows both strengths and weakness. In fiscal 2026 the company moved some sourcing from China to Vietnam and Cambodia, a response only a company that controls its own sourcing can make. In Q2 it booked $97.4 million of refunds on tariffs paid earlier, which flattered reported profit. Guidance excludes any further refund benefit. The lesson is that the model passes through some of the risk but not all of it, so margins stay exposed to trade policy and to the Canadian dollar.

Who wins from here? Aritzia wins if US demand proves durable and each new boutique matches the productivity of earlier ones. Rivals in the premium women's wear segment face a competitor that is investing roughly $1.1 billion in new store space. The AIF says the Hill Shareholders held about 65.57% of voting power as of May 6, 2026, which supports long-term investment but limits outside shareholders' say.

The main risks are concentration and fatigue. Two-thirds of revenue comes from one country that was not the home market a decade ago; a few quarters of weaker comps would test the plan. A company that targets doubling its revenue and its client base also needs the product to keep feeling special. Expanding into handbags, shoes, jewellery and beauty, expected to add about $1.5 billion of revenue, widens the opportunity but dilutes focus. For consultants, a good engagement here would test new-boutique productivity by cohort, the markdown rate by category and the return on the distribution build-out.

In a case interview you could say: Aritzia is a vertically integrated brand that earns its profit by selling its own designs at full price through large boutiques that also drive online sales, so the business depends on how much each new square foot of store sells.

What it means: Apparel retailers weighing store expansion, tariff exposure, inventory risk and omnichannel investment need growth-strategy, supply-chain and store-economics work.
