How grocery retail makes money

Confirmed · 12 min read

Open in the full site →

The short version

A grocer buys food at a wholesale price, adds a markup, and sells it again within days. Most of what it sells is perishable, shoppers buy the same basket every week, and rivals' prices sit on the shelf for anyone to compare. That makes grocery a high-volume, low-margin business: profit is the thin gap between what a retailer earns above the cost of the food and what it spends running stores, trucks, warehouses and people.

The filings show how thin the gap is. Loblaw earned C$4,424 million of operating income on C$63,903 million of 2025 revenue, a 6.9% margin (computed), helped by a large pharmacy business. Metro's adjusted net earnings were 4.8% of sales. Empire, which owns Sobeys, made adjusted net earnings of C$747 million on C$31,950 million of fiscal 2026 sales, or 2.3% (computed). Kroger, the largest US supermarket group, reported an operating profit of $1.89 billion on $147.6 billion of sales, 1.3% (computed), and about 3.0% before a $2.5 billion impairment (computed). The question for any grocer is therefore not how to get rich but how to keep a few cents on each dollar while competitors watch every price.

Private label, meaning products sold under the retailer's own name, is the best-known way to improve that arithmetic, because the retailer keeps the margin that would otherwise go to a national brand. It is also hard to measure: the Loblaw and Metro results releases I reviewed give no private label sales figure, so the strongest claims about it rest on reasoning and on US data rather than on Canadian disclosure.

How it makes money

There are three engines. The first is food sold at a markup over its cost. The second is everything else on the same footprint, especially pharmacy, health and beauty, and in some banners fuel. The third is the retailer's own brands, which lift the margin on the food itself. At Loblaw in 2025, food retail sales were C$45,234 million (70.8% of revenue, computed) and drug retail sales C$18,669 million (29.2%, computed). Food same-store sales grew 2.3%. Loblaw also reported C$335 million of operating income from discontinued operations, which is its PC Financial business, and that sits outside the C$4,424 million above.

Gross margin, the share of sales left after the cost of goods, differs widely: Loblaw 31.3%, Empire 27.1%, Kroger 22.9% and Metro 19.9%. These are not a league table. Pharmacy, beauty, fuel and the way each company classifies costs all change the figure, so the useful comparison is each company against its own past. On that basis margins are edging up: Metro 19.9% from 19.7%, Empire 27.1% from 26.8% and Kroger 22.9% from 22.3%.

Private label works because the retailer commissions a manufacturer to make the product, skips most of the national advertising a brand would carry, and controls the shelf position. In the United States, the PLMA trade body, using Circana data, reports store brands at a 21.3% dollar share of 2025 sales, up from 19.1% in 2021, and a 23.5% unit share, up from 21.6%. Unit share above dollar share means store-brand items sell at a lower average price per unit (analysis, not a reported figure). Metro's release credits what it calls strong private labels but gives no numbers.

The point for a consultant is that private label is a bargaining tool as well as a margin tool. A credible own-brand alternative weakens a national supplier's demand for higher prices and gives the grocer a way to hold its price position when shoppers trade down.

The economics

Start with Loblaw's 2025 statement, all computed from its Q4 release. Cost of sales was C$43,871 million, 68.7% of revenue, leaving gross profit of C$20,032 million, or 31.3%. Selling, general and administrative expense was C$15,608 million, 24.4% of revenue, so it absorbed 77.9% of gross profit. Operating income was C$4,424 million, 6.9%. Loblaw's retail adjusted EBITDA margin, which excludes depreciation, was 11.2%.

Metro's structure is simpler and leaner. For fiscal 2025 on sales of C$22,006.7 million, gross margin was 19.9%, operating expenses 10.4% of sales, and operating income before depreciation and similar items C$2,082.9 million, or 9.5%. Adjusted net earnings were C$1,049.8 million, 4.8% of sales, and reported net earnings were 4.6%. Empire's adjusted EBITDA margin was 7.8% for fiscal 2026, against a reported 5.5% once a C$746 million e-commerce impairment is included.

The profit pool is broad and it is growing in dollars. The Competition Bureau's 2023 market study found that the three largest grocers' profits rose from C$2.4 billion in 2019 to C$3.6 billion in 2022, and that food gross margins rose one to two percentage points since 2017, which it called modest yet meaningful (as summarised by the law firm Fasken). The Retail Council of Canada, the industry lobby, argued to the Bureau that most of the gain came from pharmacy, health and beauty and from operating efficiencies, and that food margins were flat or down. That is a claim by an interested party, but it points at a real feature: the profit pool sits partly outside the food aisle.

Behind these margins is a mostly fixed cost base. Stores, distribution centres, leases and core staffing do not shrink when volume dips, so grocers earn their return on sales growth that outruns cost growth. Same-store sales rose 2.3% at Loblaw (food, 2025), 1.9% at Empire (fiscal 2026) and 1.6% at Metro in its fourth quarter, all nominal figures that include food price inflation.

The number that runs the business

The number is gross margin, measured in single percentage points of sales. Because the final profit is only a few cents on the dollar, a one-point move in gross margin is a large share of profit. At Metro, one point of sales is about 10.5% of its 9.5% EBITDA-style margin (computed). At Kroger, one point of sales is about $1.48 billion (computed), against operating profit before the impairment of roughly $4.4 billion (computed), a little over a third.

This explains the daily behaviour of grocers. Promotions, price matching and private label launches are all attempts to protect the number from two sides at once: shoppers who will switch store for a lower basket price, and suppliers who want to push through cost increases. It also explains why the number is political. The Competition Bureau's attention to margin gains since 2017 is a reminder that regulators and customers read the same figure grocers manage, and that a rise can be seen as the system working or as pricing power.

The partner number is the operating expense ratio. Metro held operating expenses at 10.4% of sales in both fiscal 2024 and fiscal 2025 while gross margin rose by 0.2 points, and its EBITDA-style margin moved from 9.4% to 9.5%. That is the operating leverage pattern in miniature: margin gains from mix and private label only reach profit if costs stay flat.

Where AI and automation hit

The most proven uses of AI in grocery are unglamorous: forecasting demand so fewer perishables are thrown out, setting prices and promotions by store and by item, and optimising delivery routes and shelf replenishment. They work on the two numbers above, either by protecting gross margin or by holding down operating expense. I found no filing that gives a dollar figure for the savings, so treat any consultant claim of a specific percentage as an assumption until a company discloses it.

The sourced evidence in the filings is about the opposite risk, automation that outruns volume. Kroger's 2025 operating profit fell to $1.89 billion from $3.85 billion in 2024, and its release attributes $2.5 billion of the decline to impairment and related charges on its automated fulfillment network, 1.7% of sales (computed). Empire's fiscal 2026 results include a C$746 million e-commerce impairment, 2.3% of sales (computed). In both cases the write-down arrived in a single year and was large enough to cut the reported margin visibly.

The lesson is that automating a fixed-cost asset raises the break-even volume. A highly automated warehouse is efficient at full utilisation and expensive at partial use, while a store-based pick model scales more gently. Grocers therefore gain most from AI that sits on top of existing stores and supply chains, and carry the most risk from AI that needs new assets and a sudden jump in online orders.

Who wins and why

Canada is concentrated. The Competition Bureau called the industry highly concentrated and noted that at least eight large chains each had a different owner when the Competition Act was introduced in 1986, with five later bought by competitors. Loblaw, Sobeys and Metro each have over 1,000 stores, including franchised ones, and the Bureau reported their combined 2022 sales above C$100 billion. A USDA report, as cited by The Packer, puts shares at Loblaw 17%, Costco 15%, Sobeys 14%, Walmart 13% and Metro 6%, or 65% combined for the top five. That article does not state the data year, and its photo caption gives a different total of nearly 76%, so use these as an indication only. Costco and Walmart do not report Canadian sales separately.

Concentration helps explain the margin. Five chains that all see the same costs tend to move prices together without coordination, and the two price-led players, Costco and Walmart, are the main check on the others. Whoever has scale in purchasing, a pharmacy and health offer, and a trusted private label can defend gross margin; the regional or single-format grocer cannot.

On the cycle, grocery is a staple business and does not boom or bust with the economy. All three Canadian companies above kept growing same-store sales by 1.6% to 2.3%. The cycle shows up instead in mix. In the United States, store-brand unit share rose from 21.6% in 2021 to 23.5% in 2025, a period of high food inflation, which is consistent with shoppers trading down (analysis). Fresh food, pharmacy and private label are the segments that hold up; promotional national brands and discretionary general merchandise are where margin leaks.

The counter-intuitive fact is that a grocer's profit is not mainly decided in the food aisle. Loblaw's drug segment is 29.2% of revenue (computed), the Retail Council attributes much of the industry's margin gain to pharmacy, health and beauty, and the largest recent earnings shocks at Kroger and Empire came from automation write-downs, not from shoppers leaving. The winners combine a price reputation, a pharmacy that adds higher-margin sales, and a private label range that keeps the margin on the basket.

In a case interview you could say: grocery is a thin-margin, high-volume business where a point of gross margin is a third of profit, so it wins by building scale, a pharmacy and a private label range that protect that point, and by adding automation only where volume already justifies it.

What it means: Grocery is a high-volume, thin-margin business where a point of gross margin is a large share of profit. Scale, pharmacy and private label protect that point, and automation only helps where volume already justifies the fixed cost.
