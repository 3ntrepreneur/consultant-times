How Costco works

Confirmed · 12 min read

Open in the full site →

The short version

Costco sells groceries, electronics, fuel and furniture at prices that leave it very little margin, and it makes the real money elsewhere. In the 52 weeks ended August 30, 2026 (fiscal 2026), the company reported net sales of US$297.2 billion, membership fee revenue of US$5.9 billion, operating income of US$11.7 billion and net income of US$9.2 billion, according to its fourth-quarter release. Divide the fee by operating income and you get about 50.6% (computed). Half of the profit, in other words, arrives before anyone has put anything in a trolley.

That is the answer to the master question of where the profit comes from. If Costco stopped selling a particular product tomorrow, the profit would barely move. If it lost its members, almost everything would. The merchandise is the reason people pay the fee and keep paying it; the fee is the reason the merchandise can be priced so thinly.

Run the 60-second test. Who pays? The member, once a year up front, and then again at the till. Is revenue recurring? Yes, in the sense that the fee renews annually, and 92.3% of members in the U.S. and Canada did so, according to the 10-K. Is there a locked-in consumable? No. The lock-in is cheap, bulk-sized value and the sunk cost of the fee. Who owns the assets? Costco is asset-heavy: it spent US$6.4 billion on capital expenditure in fiscal 2026 and plans about US$7.5 billion in fiscal 2027, per the 10-K. What would break it? The closing section sets out the candidates.

How it makes money

Costco has two engines and a few side streams. The first is membership. Fees are recognised evenly over the one-year membership term, so the US$5,907 million booked in fiscal 2026 reflects a pool built up over the year, with US$3,006 million of fees still deferred at year-end, according to the 10-K. The annual fee in the U.S. is US$65, which includes a free household card; fees vary elsewhere. Members can upgrade to Executive for an additional fee and earn a reward of generally 2% on qualified purchases, up to an annual cap the filing does not quantify here.

The second engine is merchandise. By the 10-K's sales mix, Foods and Sundries was US$116.8 billion (about 39.3% of net sales, computed), Non-Foods US$77.4 billion (26.0%), Warehouse Ancillary and Other Businesses, which includes fuel, pharmacy, food court and travel, US$61.4 billion (20.6%) and Fresh Foods US$41.7 billion (14.0%). Core merchandise sales rose 8% in the year while ancillary and other businesses rose 20%.

The side streams matter because they feed the same loop. Fuel is priced to draw visits. Digital sales, which Costco calls digitally-enabled, exceeded US$33 billion and grew more than 20% on the earnings call, with Uber Eats and DoorDash expanding delivery partnerships. Kirkland Signature, the house brand, is a private-label engine that carries a better margin than national brands while undercutting them on shelf price. These are not separate profit pools so much as reasons to renew.

In model-catalogue terms, Costco stacks a subscription (the fee), a loss-leader pricing policy (low markups to build trust), a private-label line and a float: deferred fees and fast inventory turns mean the company holds cash, ending the year with US$20.2 billion, per the release. Stacking those four is the point.

The economics

Start with merchandise. Net sales less merchandise costs left a gross margin of US$32,968 million, or 11.09% of net sales, according to the 10-K. Selling, general and administrative costs, which cover warehouse labour, rent and utilities, were US$27,190 million, or 9.15% of net sales. Subtract one from the other and the merchandise business produced about US$5,778 million before the fee, about 1.9% of net sales (computed). The fee of US$5,907 million then roughly doubles it, to the reported operating income of US$11,685 million.

The two margins people quote are therefore both right and answer different questions. Merchandise gross margin is 11.09% of net sales. Add the fees and the margin on total revenue of US$303,154 million becomes about 12.8% (computed), but SG&A is then about 9.0% of total revenue, the figure the release implies. The honest summary is that Costco runs on a gross margin in the low teens and an expense ratio near nine percent, which is why a fraction of a percentage point of mix matters so much.

Why can Costco run SG&A at 9% of sales? Because the warehouse is a very simple machine. The 10-K says a typical core warehouse carries fewer than 4,000 active items, against 9,000 to 11,000 online. Fewer items mean larger orders per supplier, fewer decisions, faster turns and less shrink. The company reports an average warehouse sales figure of US$292 million for fiscal 2026, against US$163 million in 2017, a rise that dilutes fixed costs.

The fee also hides a cost: the Executive reward. The 10-K says the reward, netted against sales, reduced net sales by US$3,383 million in fiscal 2026, which is about 57% of fee revenue (computed). Executive members are therefore partly paid back out of the fee they paid, and in return they spend a lot. The reward is a marketing expense dressed as a discount.

Finally, watch the noise. In the fourth quarter Costco received US$184 million from tariff refunds (US$174 million of refunds plus US$10 million of interest), a non-recurring benefit of US$0.15 per diluted share after partial reinvestment in member values, according to the earnings call and the release. One-off items like this make reported margin in a single quarter a noisy guide.

The number that runs the business

It is the renewal rate. The 10-K reports 92.3% in the U.S. and Canada and 89.8% worldwide. Because the fee is half of operating income, a one-point change in renewal moves a chunk of profit. As an illustration, using the 84.1 million paid members and the roughly US$70 of fee revenue per paid member that falls out of the numbers (computed), one point of renewal is about 841,000 members and roughly US$59 million of annual fee revenue, before the spending those members would have done (computed).

The mix matters as much as the count. The 10-K reports 84.1 million paid members, up from 81.0 million, and 150.4 million cardholders including household cards. Of the paid members, 42.3 million, about 50% (computed), are Executive. According to the same filing, Executive members accounted for about 75.2% of worldwide net sales. Half the members and three-quarters of the sales: the people who pay the higher fee are also the heaviest shoppers, which makes the upgrade a self-selecting loyalty programme.

Fee increases are the second lever. The 10-K says the last U.S. and Canada increase, in September 2024, contributed about 30% of membership fee growth in fiscal 2026, so it has largely run through the numbers. The earnings call transcript, which is machine-assisted, has the CFO putting the effect at less than 1% of fee growth, so we treat the filing's 30% as the authority. A fee increase is a trust decision as much as a pricing one, which is why it is made rarely.

The pricing policy is what protects the renewal rate: Costco prices to keep members' trust rather than to extract the most from each basket, and the fee is the return on that restraint.

Where AI and automation hit

Costco's own filings describe AI mostly as an exposure. The 10-K cites it in several places, for example noting that the rapid evolution of AI has automated and accelerated the scale of cybersecurity threats. It does not quantify any AI savings.

Where AI shows up as an opportunity is the online channel. Management said on the earnings call that traffic from AI search grew at triple-digit rates for a second straight quarter and converts better than other traffic. That is a small part of a channel (digitally-enabled sales above US$33 billion, up more than 20%) that is itself a minority of a US$297 billion business.

The physical model limits automation. Few items, pallet-level handling and customers who do their own picking already strip out labour that other grocers spend on shelving and choice. The filing counts 355,000 employees, about 95% in warehouses and distribution, with 58,000 of them in Canada. Analysts should watch for automation in depots and online fulfilment, not in the warehouse floor, and for AI tools that compare Costco prices on the member's behalf and weaken the habit of simply going.

Who wins and why

Costco's moat is scale combined with trust. The scale lets it buy fewer items in enormous volume; the trust lets it keep prices low without a fight over every receipt, because members have prepaid. Rivals can copy the shed, but copying the bargain is harder, because their own profit is in the markup and Costco's is in the fee.

Kirkland Signature is the clearest example of vertical logic. Management said on the earnings call that Kirkland items save members "at least 15% to 20%" against national brands, and it cut prices on several lines in the fourth quarter, such as walnuts from US$13.79 to US$9.99. The 10-K says the company expects to keep raising private-label penetration, though the sections we read gave no share-of-sales figure, so we do not state one. The same filing flags a risk: Kirkland quality and brand integrity matter, and it discloses class actions over Kirkland tequila labelling.

Canada is Costco's second-largest market and its best-performing by profitability. Canada had 115 of the 939 warehouses at year-end (about 12%, computed), 58,000 employees, and a segment revenue of US$40,561 million with operating income of US$2,107 million, per the 10-K. That is an operating margin of about 5.2% against about 3.5% in the U.S. and 4.5% in Other International (all computed from the segment table), and Canada produced about 18% of group operating income from about 13% of revenue (computed). Canadian comparable sales rose 7.8% in fiscal 2026 (6.7% excluding gasoline and currency), according to the Q4 release.

Expansion continues. Costco plans 33 openings in fiscal 2027, five of them in Canada, according to the earnings call. Retail Insider reports Canadian projects in Wasaga Beach, Lloydminster, East Windsor, Regina and Rocky View County due in late 2026, with Thunder Bay, Halton Hills, South Surrey and Oakville targeted for 2027, and Business Centres opened in New Westminster, B.C. in November 2025 and Winnipeg in March 2026. Timings are the developers' or Retail Insider's estimates, not Costco commitments. Costco says it is aiming for about 30 net new warehouses a year.

What could break it? First, trust: a clumsy fee increase or a visible drop in value would show up in renewal before it shows up in sales. Second, concentration: the 10-K flags reliance on the U.S. and Canada, and California alone is 26% of U.S. net sales. Third, cost: tariffs, inflation and the LIFO charge pressure margin even when refunds flatter a quarter. Fourth, competition and AI-driven shopping changes, which the filing names. Fifth, expansion: new warehouses can cannibalise old ones, as the 10-K itself warns. None of these is imminent, and the numbers are strong: fees up about 11% (computed from US$5,907 million against US$5,323 million) and paid members up 3.8% according to the earnings call. But a business that earns half its profit from a promise is only as strong as the promise.

What it means: Costco is a membership business that happens to sell goods at near-cost prices. Retailers, consumer-goods suppliers and private-equity investors will hire consultants to benchmark membership economics, private-label strategy and the pressure Costco's price position puts on rivals.
