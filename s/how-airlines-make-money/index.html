How airlines make money

Confirmed · 14 min read

Open in the full site →

The short version

An airline sells a seat on a specific flight, and once the door closes an empty seat is worth nothing. The aircraft, the pilots, the fuel to get airborne and the landing fees are nearly the same whether the plane carries 120 people or 180. That makes airlines a fixed-cost, perishable-inventory business, and almost everything interesting about them follows from that: pricing is a daily auction, load factor is the master dial, and a small change in revenue or fuel swings profit by a lot.

The other defining fact is how little is left at the end. IATA, the global airline trade body, forecast in June 2026 that the industry will earn $23.0 billion of net profit on $1.165 trillion of revenue, a 2.0% net margin and about $4.50 for each passenger carried. That forecast was cut roughly in half after the Middle East conflict pushed up jet fuel; before the shock, IATA had projected $41 billion. IATA also notes that the industry has never posted a net margin above 5%. So the question for any airline is not how to get rich, but how to stay solidly above zero through the cycle.

How it makes money

Most revenue is still the ticket. Air Canada's 2025 annual report shows C$22,372 million of operating revenue, of which passenger revenue was C$19,604 million (87.6%, computed), cargo C$1,033 million (4.6%, computed) and other revenue C$1,735 million (7.8%, computed). Across the whole industry, IATA expects 2026 revenue of $839 billion from passenger tickets, $165 billion from ancillary items such as bags and seat selection, and $162 billion from cargo. Ancillaries are growing fastest, at 12.6%, against 9.2% for tickets.

The more telling story is inside the ticket and beside it. Delta's second-quarter 2026 release splits its $19.8 billion of revenue in detail. Premium tickets brought in $6.9 billion and main-cabin tickets $6.85 billion, so premium cabins are now slightly larger than economy as a share of revenue (35.0% against 34.7%, computed). Delta also reports that revenue streams other than main-cabin tickets (premium, loyalty, cargo, maintenance and so on) are 61% of its adjusted revenue, up from 59% a year earlier. Its refinery line is an odd extra: $2.1 billion of third-party fuel sales that carry matching cost, so it inflates revenue without adding much profit.

The loyalty programme is the part that behaves least like an airline. Delta received $2.4 billion from American Express in the quarter, up 16%. The card issuer pays the airline for points that it then hands to cardholders, so the airline is selling a financial product, not a seat. Air Canada's Aeroplan works the same way, and Air Canada used proceeds from an Aeroplan transaction to repay C$1.7 billion of debt and fund a C$800 million share buyback, according to its Q3 2026 release. The release does not disclose the transaction's size, so treat this as evidence of the programme's value, not a number to extrapolate from.

Cargo is a smaller but useful stream. Air Canada's cargo revenue was up 33% in Q3 2026, and Delta's grew 39% in Q2, but both are well under 5% of revenue at these carriers, so cargo helps at the margin rather than defining the model.

The economics

Start with the cost stack. Air Canada's 2025 expenses total C$21,454 million. The largest lines are wages, salaries and benefits at C$5,005 million (23.3% of costs, computed) and aircraft fuel at C$4,731 million (22.1%, computed). Next come depreciation (C$2,012 million), airport and navigation fees (C$1,587 million), aircraft maintenance (C$1,343 million), sales and distribution (C$1,075 million), ground packages (C$872 million) and regional capacity purchase fees (C$863 million). Operating income was C$918 million, a 4.1% operating margin (computed). That is a normal year for a good airline.

The industry view from IATA is similar but fuel-heavy in 2026. Total operating expenses are forecast at $1.117 trillion, of which fuel is $350 billion (31.4%, up from 25.4% in 2025) and labour is $271 billion (about 24%, computed). Non-fuel costs are growing a modest 4.0%, while fuel is up nearly 40%. IATA's assumptions explain why: Brent crude averages $95 a barrel (against $69 in 2025), jet fuel averages $152 a barrel (against $90), and the gap between jet fuel and crude, the crack spread, is at a historic high of $57 a barrel. Only about one-third of 2026 fuel use is hedged.

The Air Canada quarter shows what that does to a P&L. In Q3 2026, revenue rose 21% to C$6,984 million, yet operating income was only C$261 million, a 3.7% margin against 4.9% a year earlier. Fuel cost C$2,118 million, which is C$906 million more than a year ago and about 30% of revenue (computed). Fuel cost per litre rose to 148.3 cents from 88.3 cents. The fuel increase alone was about 3.5 times the quarter's operating income (computed). Management responded by cutting its full-year adjusted EBITDA guidance to C$2.4 to 2.7 billion from C$2.9 to 3.2 billion, and said that only about 70% of extra Q4 fuel cost would be recovered through fares and surcharges.

Delta shows the same pressure with a stronger starting point. Its September quarter operating margin was 7.2% on a GAAP basis (9.4% adjusted), and it says it has absorbed about $6 billion of higher fuel cost in 2026. Smaller, leisure-heavy Air Transat is on the sharp end: its fiscal third quarter to July 31 produced a C$106.6 million loss, with about C$105 million of extra fuel cost in the quarter, and it said its ability to pass costs to customers had been very limited. Ottawa has stepped in with federal financing for Transat and a federal loan for Porter.

Behind these costs is a supply problem. IATA says aircraft are scarce: the order backlog hit 18,100 in May 2026, more than half the active fleet, lease rates are at record levels, and older planes cost more to maintain. That is why airlines cannot simply grow their way out. Fewer planes keep competitors honest on capacity, but they also keep costs high.

The number that runs the business

The number is load factor: the share of seats that carry a paying passenger. Because the cost of flying is mostly fixed, the last few seats filled drop almost straight to profit, and the last few left empty are pure loss. IATA expects a record 84.0% industry load factor in 2026. Air Canada's was 89.1% in Q3 2026, up 2.6 points, in the peak summer quarter.

Load factor cannot be read alone, because an airline can fill a plane by selling cheap. The partner number is yield, the revenue earned per passenger-mile flown. Air Canada's yield rose 11.0% to 23.8 cents in Q3, so it filled more seats and charged more per mile at the same time, which is the combination that pays for a fuel shock. The summary is revenue per available seat-mile (roughly load factor times yield) set against cost per available seat-mile. Air Canada's Q3 unit revenue was 23.4 cents against a GAAP unit cost of 22.5 cents, which leaves a thin gap. Excluding fuel and one-off items, its adjusted unit cost was 14.45 cents, up only 3.3%, which shows that the squeeze came almost entirely from fuel.

That thin gap is why airlines obsess over capacity discipline. When one carrier adds seats on a route, rivals match, fares fall and everyone's load factor-yield trade-off worsens. In the autumn of 2026, Canadian commentators were already predicting a price war once the summer peak faded, with some smaller carriers at risk, which is a live example of the problem.

Where AI and automation hit

The most mature use of AI in aviation is revenue management: algorithms that decide, flight by flight and hour by hour, how many seats to sell at each fare bucket and when to raise prices. This directly attacks the master dial, because a one-point gain in load factor or yield is worth more than most cost programmes. Close behind are crew and fleet scheduling tools that re-plan a network when weather or a strike hits, and predictive maintenance that flags parts before they fail, which matters when older aircraft are being flown longer. Airport self-service and automated customer messaging take out some sales and service labour.

These tools help at the edges, but IATA's own numbers show the limits. Labour costs are forecast to rise 4.0% in 2026 while workforce productivity per employee falls 0.4%, because airlines are flying older fleets with fewer new aircraft and tight crew supply. Fuel, the biggest swing cost, cannot be automated away. For a consultant, the right question is not whether AI saves cost, but whether it raises revenue per seat or protects reliability enough to matter against a fuel bill that moved by hundreds of millions of dollars in one quarter.

Who wins and why

Profit pools are uneven. Within the industry, IATA expects North American airlines to earn $9.4 billion in 2026 (a 2.5% net margin and $8.10 per passenger), down from $12.4 billion in 2025. That is better per passenger than the global $4.50, which tells you that scale, strong home markets and premium and loyalty revenue help. Outside the airlines, the owners of scarce things also do well: aircraft lessors at record lease rates and card issuers that pay for loyalty points sit in structurally healthier positions than the airline itself.

Concentration shapes who can pass costs on. In Canada, the Competition Bureau describes the domestic market as highly concentrated with two leading carriers. A June 2025 summary put Air Canada and WestJet at 56% to 78% of domestic passengers at the eight busiest airports, with a figure of up to 78% on key routes. Air Canada competes with WestJet on about 61% of its routes, down from 72% in 2019, and the bureau estimates one more competitor on a route is associated with 9% lower prices. Two dominant carriers can pass fuel costs on more easily than a sector of many small ones, but the Air Transat and Porter cases show that smaller, leisure-focused or less diversified carriers hit trouble first.

The cycle is the other half of the story. Airline profits move with GDP, consumer confidence and fuel. IATA's 2025 numbers (net profit of $45 billion on a 4.2% margin) were described as thin even in a good year, and 2026 halved them within a few months. Counter-cyclical cushions exist but are small: cargo, loyalty income that arrives whether or not planes are full, and sale-leasebacks, which Air Canada is using to raise cash (C$501 million so far in 2026, with an assumed C$1 billion for the year). Air Canada ended Q3 with C$8,018 million of liquidity and net debt of 1.3 times EBITDA, which is the kind of cushion that decides who survives a bad winter.

The counter-intuitive fact is that the industry earns less than its cost of capital even in good years. IATA puts 2026 return on invested capital at 4.3%, against a weighted average cost of capital of 8.5%, so the industry as a whole is destroying value by its own measure even while it reports a profit. The carriers that beat that gap do it by acting less like airlines and more like diversified businesses: Delta's premium cabins and American Express income, Air Canada's Aeroplan, cargo and maintenance work.

In a case interview you could say: an airline is a fixed-cost, perishable-seat business with a margin of a few percent, so it wins by filling planes at good prices, selling premium cabins and loyalty points, and staying liquid enough to survive the next fuel spike.

What it means: Airlines are a high-fixed-cost, near-commodity business where the margin ceiling is set by fuel, labour and aircraft supply. The winners are the carriers that fill planes, sell premium seats and turn their loyalty programme into a second business.
