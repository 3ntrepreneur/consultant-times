America's Car-Mart files Chapter 11 with $10 million stalking-horse bid

Reported · 4 min

Open in the full site →

America's Car-Mart, a subprime used-car dealer and lender, filed for Chapter 11 protection in the Southern District of Texas on Tuesday 24 November 2026, according to Bloomberg Law and TheStreet. The filing lists estimated assets of $100 million to $500 million and liabilities of $1 billion to $10 billion, which are ranges, not exact figures.

TheStreet reports that the company proposes a court-supervised sale of substantially all its assets to Cardinal Asset Holdings and affiliates as a stalking-horse bidder, meaning an opening offer that other bidders can top. The consideration includes $10 million of base cash, $13.6 million for owned real estate, inventory-based payments, rollover debt and assumed liabilities. The company also seeks up to $60 million of new debtor-in-possession loans and up to $65 million of roll-up loans. Both the sale and the financing need bankruptcy court approval.

According to TheStreet, dealerships are expected to stay open, customers should keep paying on their current schedule, and service and accident protection plans are expected to continue, subject to court approval. The proposed plan would pay general unsecured claims in full, subject to approval, and give common stockholders $2.5 million from excluded assets with possible further recovery; no recovery is guaranteed. The report says Cardinal is led by executives with automotive retail and consumer finance experience, including a former Car-Mart chief executive and chairman.

The filing follows a long contraction that the company disclosed in stages. For the fiscal year ended 30 April 2026, Car-Mart reported revenue of $1.28 billion, down 7.9%, and a net loss of $139.1 million, or $16.79 per share, according to its July results release and Talk Business. Retail units fell 14.3% for the year and 27.1% in the fourth quarter, net charge-offs reached 27.6% of average receivables from 25.9%, and the allowance for credit losses stood at 25.15% of receivables. Active dealerships fell from 154 to 94, a cut of about 40%, as 42 were consolidated in the fourth quarter alone. The company's annual report carried a going-concern warning, and chief executive Douglas Campbell said at the time that it had not yet secured the financing or alternative transaction needed to resolve its liquidity constraint, according to Bloomberg Law.

The loan book showed the strain. In the year to 30 April 2026, active customers fell 6.7% to 97,696, accounts more than 30 days past due rose to 4.1% from 3.4%, and the average contract term was 49 months, according to Talk Business and the company's release. Car-Mart's chief financial officer, Jonathan Collins, left at the end of July to join another company, and a capital-markets executive took over the role, Talk Business reports.

The company described the problem as a liquidity and capital-structure issue and not a credit-quality one. At 30 April it had total debt of $722.4 million against $1.41 billion of finance receivables, and unrestricted cash of $47.0 million. A $300 million term loan closed in October 2025, and on 19 June 2026 the company agreed an amendment with those lenders that gave covenant relief and a defined window to review strategic and financing alternatives. A special committee ran that review.

The numbers worsened after the review began. In the quarter ended 31 July 2026, reported on 9 September, retail units fell 81.9% to 2,450, total revenue fell 57.3% to $145.8 million, and the net loss was $69.0 million, or $8.28 per share. Gross margin dropped to 21.8% from 36.6%, wholesale sales rose to 23.4% of sales as inventory was run down to $35.2 million, and principal receivables fell 21.4% to $1.19 billion. Net charge-offs rose to 9.5% for the quarter and unrestricted cash fell to $27.5 million. The company also booked $13.7 million of non-recurring costs, including $9.6 million of professional fees tied to the strategic review. On 4 September it extended the lender amendment's termination date to 11 September. These figures come from the company's own releases, which were partly summarised in the pages reviewed.

Not yet known are the names of the advisers, whether competing bids will appear, how the court will treat the proposed financing, and what happens to the customer loan portfolio that borrowers continue to pay into.

What it means: Restructuring, DIP-financing, receivables-portfolio valuation and turnaround work for the company and its lenders, and credit-risk reviews for other subprime auto lenders and dealers.
