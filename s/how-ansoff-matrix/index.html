The Ansoff matrix: four ways to grow

Confirmed · 12 min read

Open in the full site →

The short version

Most growth conversations in a boardroom are really a pile of unrelated ideas: enter a new country, launch a new line, cut prices, buy a competitor. The Ansoff matrix is a way to sort that pile. It asks only two questions of any idea. Is the product something the company already sells, or something new to it? Is the customer or market one the company already serves, or a new one? Two questions with two answers each give four boxes, and each box carries a different kind of risk.

Igor Ansoff set this out in a 1957 Harvard Business Review article, "Strategies for Diversification". The Wikipedia entry on the matrix cites it as volume 35, issue 5, pages 113-124 (September-October 1957); we did not open the original. The four routes are market penetration (existing product, existing market), market development (existing product, new market), product development (new product, existing market) and diversification (new product, new market). Penetration means selling more of the same to the same people, through price, promotion, distribution or retention. Market development takes the existing range to new countries, channels or customer groups. Product development gives existing customers something new to buy. Diversification does both at once.

The matrix is popular with consultants because it is cheap and forces a choice of words. It will not tell you which idea is best. What it does is stop a team treating a new-country launch and a new-category launch as the same kind of risk, and it gives the discussion a common vocabulary before the numbers arrive. The sensible reading is the one a practitioner page at Umbrex gives: the grid is the beginning of strategy, and without customer evidence and economics it becomes "a slogan rather than a strategy".

How to do it properly

Start by defining the two axes in one sentence each. A product is not a brand or a business unit; it is whatever the customer actually buys, at the level of detail that matters for capability. A market is a defined set of customers reachable through a defined set of channels. If the team cannot agree whether women's leggings sold to men are a new product or a new market, the matrix will produce an argument, not a decision. Umbrex lists vague definitions as the first pitfall, with teams misclassifying moves and debates going in circles.

Second, build a backlog of real ideas rather than filling the boxes with generalities. Each idea gets a one-line hypothesis and a quadrant. Ideas that straddle two quadrants should be split into pieces that can be tested separately: opening stores in a new country is market development, but launching a locally designed range there is product development layered on top.

Third, order by risk, but do it carefully. The usual ranking puts penetration lowest, market development and product development in the middle, and diversification highest, because each step away from the origin adds something the firm has not done before. Wikipedia's summary of the framework says exactly this and treats the two middle routes as similar in risk. Umbrex adds the necessary caveat: the risk gradient "generally increases" away from penetration, but it is not a law of nature and can mislead. A close segment next door can be safer than a complicated new product, and a penetration push into a hostile, price-war market can be riskier than it looks. Capability fit, industry structure and unit economics decide, not the box alone.

Fourth, test each idea on four things: the size of the prize (use a serviceable market, not a headline one), the capability gap (what the firm would have to build, buy or borrow), the economics (investment, time to revenue, cannibalisation of the existing business) and the evidence that customers want it. Umbrex's guidance is to keep a balanced portfolio, with most effort in penetration and near adjacencies and a small share in diversification. That 50-70% (penetration and near adjacencies) / 20-40% (product and market development) / 10-20% (diversification options) split is one practitioner's rule of thumb, not a law, but it captures the point that the core has to keep earning while the options are explored.

A worked example

Lululemon is a useful test case because its latest numbers show all of the matrix's tensions at once. The figures below come from its second-quarter fiscal 2026 release (quarter ended 2 August 2026, published 3 September 2026), its fourth-quarter and full-year fiscal 2025 release (17 March 2026) and its fiscal 2025 annual report on Form 10-K (year ended 1 February 2026). All amounts are in US dollars.

Start with where the company sits. In fiscal 2025 the Americas generated US$7.847 billion of revenue, down 1.0% on the year, which was 71% of the total. China Mainland produced US$1.755 billion, up 28.9%, and the Rest of World (Asia-Pacific plus Europe, Middle East and Africa) US$1.501 billion, up 15.6%, according to the 10-K. Adding the last two gives international revenue of about US$3.256 billion, around 29% of the total (computed). International revenue grew by about US$596 million over fiscal 2024's US$2.660 billion, while the Americas shrank by about US$81 million (both computed from the 10-K). In other words, all of the company's net growth in fiscal 2025 came from outside the home region.

That is market development at work, and it is expensive in the way the framework predicts: it needs stores. The 10-K shows 811 company-operated stores at 1 February 2026, up 44 on the year, with 476 in the Americas, 172 in China Mainland (up from 151) and 163 in the Rest of World (up from 154). Mexico went from 17 stores to 26. Management said in the 10-K that it expects most 2026 openings to be in China Mainland, and the company also plans stores in new markets in Europe and Asia-Pacific. On the second-quarter call, management mentioned franchise stores in Athens and Bucharest and a Zalando marketing collaboration across 12 European markets, according to a call transcript published by Webull.

The second-quarter numbers show the limit of that route. Total revenue fell 4% to US$2.416 billion (down 5% in constant dollars), with the Americas down 8% and international up 4% (up 2% in constant dollars), according to Lululemon's release. China Mainland rose 4% as reported but fell 2% in constant currency, with comparable sales down 8% in constant currency. The Rest of World rose 5%. So the growth region slowed sharply at the same moment the core region weakened. Management blamed negative brand commentary and a different promotional calendar in China, according to the call transcript, which is reported speech rather than an audited fact.

Now the core, which is a penetration problem. Americas comparable sales fell 12% in the quarter. Management's stated priorities, per the transcript, are returning to full-price sales growth, cutting store SKU density by 15%, raising marketing in the second half and trimming pop-up shops from 65 to about 40. Those are classic penetration levers: reach, price realisation and retention in the existing market rather than new territory.

Product development is the third route. Men's was 24% of fiscal 2025 revenue and women's 63%, with accessories and other at 13%, according to the 10-K, which calls men's "a key pillar of our strategic growth plans". On the second-quarter call, men's revenue fell about 1%, leggings about 20% and accessories 13%, as reported by Webull's transcript. Whether men's is product development or market development is a fair argument, since it is both a new range and a new customer group, which is exactly why definitions matter. The new product chief appointed on 7 October 2026, Maggie Gauger, is to oversee design, merchandising, footwear and product innovation, according to the company's announcement. We found no footwear revenue figures in the sources we read, so we do not size that route.

On diversification, the sources we read show no active unrelated bet; the company's moves are all adjacent. The strategic reset is under way: interim co-CEOs ran the second quarter, new chief executive Heidi O'Neill was due to join the following week, and the company cut its fiscal 2026 revenue outlook to US$10.35-10.50 billion, a 5% to 7% decline, from earlier guidance of US$11.0-11.15 billion (just-style). The third-quarter outlook is for revenue down 10% to 11%. As of 16 November 2026 the third-quarter results had not been published.

From analysis to choices

A matrix is a classification, not a recommendation. The step that turns it into choices is a scored shortlist. Put each candidate idea in a row and give it the same columns: the prize in revenue and profit, the investment, the months to first revenue, the capability gap, the cannibalisation risk and the evidence so far. For Lululemon the shortlist might read: restore Americas full-price sales (penetration), keep opening in China and Europe (market development), grow men's and accessories (product development) and footwear (product development, size unknown). Each row then gets a different test.

Penetration is tested against the health of the core. If Americas comparable sales are down 12%, the question is whether the cause is product, price, brand sentiment or competition, because the fix differs. Adding new things to a broken core often just spreads the problem. Market development is tested on payback by market: the 172 stores in China Mainland produced US$1.755 billion, roughly US$10 million per store, against about US$16.5 million per store in the Americas (both computed crudely from the 10-K, since revenue includes online and store counts are year-end). That is a crude comparison that a consultant would refine with store-level data, but it frames the question of whether new-market stores earn their cost.

Product development is tested on cannibalisation and attach rates: does a new category bring new buyers, or only move existing ones around? Diversification, if on the list at all, is tested as an option: a small, time-limited pilot with explicit stop rules. Umbrex recommends managing the larger bets as options that scale with evidence, not all-or-nothing wagers.

Finally, sequence and own. The output of the exercise is a short list of two or three priorities, with an owner, a leading indicator and a review date for each quadrant. A good readout also states what the company will not do, which is where the matrix earns its keep: it makes a rejected idea easy to explain.

Common mistakes

The most frequent error is sloppy definitions: classing a move in whichever box makes it sound safest. If an idea needs a new customer and a new product, call it diversification and price its risk that way. A related mistake is inflating the new market. A headline market size for a country or category is not what the firm can actually reach with its channels and brand, and Umbrex points to inflated market definitions as a reason new-market results disappoint.

Second, ignoring capability fit. Moving from selling online to running stores, or from clothing to footwear, calls for different skills in sourcing, fit, inventory and service. The matrix says nothing about whether the firm can do it. Third, forgetting that the boxes interact. A new product can cannibalise the core, and a new market can strain the supply chain that serves the old one. Lululemon's second-quarter inventory was US$1.711 billion, down 1% on the year, but units were down 7% (release), a reminder that more complexity in range and geography has to be carried in stock.

Fourth, penetration myopia: pushing the core harder with discounting. Lululemon's management said markdowns rose 70 basis points in the quarter (call transcript), and the general risk of discounting is real, because discounts buy volume at the cost of brand and margin. Finally, treating the matrix as the answer. It cannot tell a board whether the core is healthy, whether the economics work or whether the company can afford the bet. It simply stops the conversation from skipping those questions.

Where AI and automation hit

AI is most useful at the front and back of the process. At the front, it can take a long list of ideas from interviews, board papers and market reports, tag them to quadrants and flag the ones that straddle two. It can pull public numbers such as regional revenue, store counts and comparable sales from filings into a consistent table in minutes, and draft first-pass market sizing and unit economics with ranges. At the back, it can monitor the leading indicators for each quadrant and flag when one drifts.

The middle is where it is weak. Judging capability fit means knowing how an organisation actually works, such as whether its merchants can design a footwear range or whether its country managers can run franchise partners, and that knowledge is rarely written down. Choosing the acceptable level of risk is a decision about the owners' money and a board's patience. AI also reproduces whatever definitions it is given, so a muddled product or market definition will be sorted confidently into the wrong box. The practical rule is to use automation to widen the list and speed the numbers, then have people decide what is a product, what is a market and what the firm is prepared to lose.

What it means: Case-interview use: for any 'how do we grow?' question, sort ideas into the four quadrants, size each, test capability fit, and recommend a sequence with stop rules.
