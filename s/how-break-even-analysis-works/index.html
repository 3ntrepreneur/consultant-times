Break-even analysis: how many sales before a business stops losing money

Confirmed · 11 min read

Open in the full site →

The short version

Every business pays some bills whether or not anyone buys anything, and some bills only when it sells. Break-even analysis separates the two and asks one question: how much must we sell before the first group of bills is covered? Below that level the business loses money; above it, each extra sale adds profit.

Four terms do the work. Fixed costs do not move with volume in the short run: rent, salaried staff, aircraft ownership. Variable costs rise with each unit sold: materials, delivery, card fees. Contribution margin is the selling price minus the variable cost, the slice of each sale left to pay the fixed costs and then to become profit. The break-even point is the sales volume at which total contribution equals total fixed costs. Corporate Finance Institute gives the formulas: break-even units equal fixed costs divided by contribution margin per unit, and break-even revenue is that figure times the price, or fixed costs divided by the contribution margin ratio. Its worked example has a $60 price, $45 variable cost and $240,000 of fixed costs, giving a $15 contribution, a 25% ratio and 16,000 units, or $960,000 of revenue.

Two companion ideas matter just as much. The margin of safety is the cushion between actual sales and the break-even level, in other words how far sales can fall before losses begin. Operating leverage describes how sharply profit reacts to a change in sales. OpenStax's managerial accounting text defines the degree of operating leverage as contribution margin divided by operating income, and says a firm with high fixed costs gains more when sales rise and loses faster when they fall. Air Canada's latest quarter, below, shows exactly that.

How to do it properly

Start with the cost split, because everything else depends on it. List every cost line and ask what happens to it if volume rises by one unit, one customer or one flight. Costs that do not move belong in fixed. Costs that move belong in variable. Many are neither: a salaried manager is fixed until a second shift is needed, then the cost jumps. Treat these as step costs and note where each assumption holds. Corporate Finance Institute's guide implies, without saying so outright, that the analysis only works if costs can be cleanly split in this way, and that the hardest part is estimating how sales patterns will respond to changes.

Next, define the unit. For a bakery it is a loaf; for a software firm a subscriber-month; for an airline, an available seat-mile flown and filled. The unit must be something that carries one price and one variable cost. If a company sells five products, work out the break-even on a weighted-average contribution margin for the usual sales mix, and state the mix you assumed, because a shift toward the low-margin product moves the break-even even if total sales are unchanged.

Then compute in three steps. First, contribution per unit: price minus variable cost. Second, break-even units: total fixed costs divided by that contribution. Third, translate into revenue and time, for example dividing by expected monthly sales to see how many months to cover a launch cost.

Finally, read the answer against reality. Compare break-even with realistic sales and compute the margin of safety, using OpenStax's definition: the shortfall of sales from break-even divided by sales. Then compute the degree of operating leverage and use it as a sensitivity multiplier: the percentage change in operating income is roughly the leverage figure times the percentage change in sales. Run it up and down.

A worked example

Air Canada is a good test because an airline is close to a pure fixed-cost machine and, this autumn, was hit by a large shock to its biggest variable cost. All figures are in Canadian dollars from the company's third-quarter 2026 results release unless marked computed or assumption. The release's own outlook was updated on 29 October 2026.

The bare results first. Third-quarter operating revenue was C$6,984 million, against C$5,774 million a year earlier. Operating expenses were C$6,723 million, against C$5,490 million. Operating income was therefore C$261 million, down from C$284 million, an operating margin of 3.7% against 4.9%. Capacity, measured in available seat miles, rose 5.7% to 29,893 million, and traffic, in revenue passenger miles, rose 8.9% to 26,642 million. The passenger load factor, the share of seats filled, rose from 86.5% to 89.1%. Passenger revenue per mile, or yield, rose 11.0% to 23.8 cents.

Airlines use a version of break-even analysis called the break-even load factor: the share of seats that must be filled to cover costs. The airline does not report one in this release, so we compute a rough one. If costs are set by the flying schedule, and revenue scales with seats filled, the break-even load factor is the actual load factor multiplied by the ratio of costs to revenue. For the third quarter of 2026 that is 89.1% times 6,723 over 6,984, or about 85.8% (computed). A year earlier it was 86.5% times 5,490 over 5,774, or about 82.2% (computed). It rests on a strong simplification, namely that cargo and other revenue move in step with passenger traffic and that no cost varies with passengers, so treat it as an indicator and not a reported measure.

Read it as a margin of safety. In 2025 the airline filled 86.5% of seats against a break-even near 82.2%, a cushion of about 4.3 points (computed). In 2026 it filled 89.1% against about 85.8%, a cushion of about 3.3 points (computed). Higher occupancy bought a smaller cushion, because the break-even itself rose by about 3.5 points (computed). That is the story of the quarter: the airline worked its planes harder and still earned less.

The reason is fuel. Aircraft fuel expense was C$2,118 million against C$1,212 million, up C$906 million, and the fuel price per litre was 148.3 cents against 88.3 cents. Fuel was 31.5% of operating expenses against 22.1% a year earlier (computed). Fuel per available seat mile rose from about 4.3 cents to about 7.1 cents (computed). Strip fuel out and the rest of the cost base rose far more slowly, from about 15.1 cents to about 15.4 cents per available seat mile (computed). The nine-month picture is thinner still: operating income of C$163 million on revenue of C$19,035 million, a 0.9% margin against 3.6%, and a load factor of 87.7% against 84.6%. On the same method the nine-month break-even load factor is about 86.9% (computed).

Now add leverage, with a clearly labelled assumption. Transportgeography.org, drawing on United States airline data for 2019, says about two-thirds of airline operating expenses are fixed. Assume, purely for illustration, that the same split applies to Air Canada's quarter (assumption). Then fixed costs are about C$4,482 million and variable costs about C$2,241 million. Contribution is C$4,743 million, a ratio near 68%, and the break-even revenue is about C$6,599 million, a margin of safety of about 5.5% (all computed under that assumption). The degree of operating leverage is 4,743 over 261, about 18 (computed). On those assumptions a 1% fall in sales would cut operating income by roughly 18%. The split is not Air Canada's, but the direction is clear: a few points of lost demand can wipe out a quarter's profit.

The outlook shows the same exposure. Air Canada cut its 2026 adjusted EBITDA guidance to C$2.4 to 2.7 billion from C$2.9 to 3.2 billion, and expects free cash flow of about break-even, plus or minus C$150 million. It blamed higher expected fuel prices. For the fourth quarter it assumes jet fuel at C$1.72 a litre, up from C$1.29, and expects to offset about 70% of the incremental cost, against 100% before. Across the industry, the International Air Transport Association's June 2026 update puts 2026 net profit at US$23.0 billion, a 2.0% margin, against an earlier forecast near US$41 billion, with fuel at 31.4% of costs against 25.4% in 2025 and a passenger load factor forecast of 84.0%.

From analysis to choices

Break-even analysis becomes useful when it points at levers. There are only four. Raise the price, which lifts contribution on every unit but may cut volume. Cut variable cost per unit. Cut fixed costs. Raise volume. Each lever moves the break-even in a different way and has a different price, and the job of the analyst is to say which is cheapest and most reversible.

Air Canada's quarter shows all four. Price: yield rose 11.0%, so the airline passed a large part of the fuel shock to customers, and load factor rose too, which is unusual. That is a strength of the franchise, and also a limit, since fares cannot be raised indefinitely. Volume: capacity was up 5.7% in the quarter, but management has said the full year will be about 2.75% above 2025, so the volume lever has been used about as far as the schedule allows. Fixed cost: aircraft, crews and airport costs are committed for the season, so the choice is long-term, for instance deferring deliveries or retiring older aircraft. Variable cost: fuel is the obvious target, but it moves with flying, not with passengers. The company's statement that about half of its fourth-quarter fuel needs is exposed to price swings points to hedging and fuel-efficient flying as the realistic levers.

A second use is the go or no-go test on a new venture. Before launching a route, a store or a product line, compute its stand-alone break-even and compare it with a plausible ramp-up. If break-even volume needs a share of the market no competitor has won, the idea is probably a stretch, whatever the strategic story says.

Finally, set a minimum margin of safety and name the trigger that forces action, such as a fuel price or a load factor. A break-even nobody monitors is a slide, not a control.

Common mistakes

The first error is a sloppy cost split. Labelling a cost fixed because it is awkward to trace, or variable because it is easy, can shift the answer by large amounts. Fuel is a good illustration. It is the biggest variable cost for an airline, but it varies with flights, not with passengers, so the cost of carrying one more passenger on a flight already scheduled is tiny. Transportgeography.org makes the same point, saying that most costs are fixed and the marginal cost of an extra passenger is very small, which encourages discounting of unsold seats near departure. Analysts who treat fuel as variable per passenger will get the wrong break-even for a single flight.

The second error is treating the model as exact. The classic version assumes one price, a constant variable cost per unit, fixed costs that stay fixed across the whole range and a stable product mix. In practice discounts grow with volume, a second shift adds cost and customers trade down, so check the model at the volume you expect. The airline example carries the same warning. Our break-even load factor ignores the mix of cabins, cargo and routes, and any hedging gains.

Third, ignoring time and cash. Break-even compares revenue and costs in one period. It does not tell you when you recoup the upfront investment, or whether you run out of cash first. Fourth, forgetting that the cushion shrinks quietly. Air Canada's load factor hit a high and its margin of safety still narrowed, because costs rose faster than occupancy. Watch the break-even level as closely as sales.

Last, worshipping a benchmark. The two-thirds fixed-cost share we borrowed from a United States 2019 chart is a reference point, not a fact about Air Canada. Ask for the company's own cost data before relying on it.

Where AI and automation hit

AI is well suited to the mechanical parts. It can extract revenue, cost and volume figures from filings into a consistent table, propose a first-pass fixed and variable split, compute break-even, margin of safety and operating leverage, and run sensitivity grids for price, volume and fuel in seconds. It can also flag when load factor, yield or fuel moves the break-even past a set threshold.

It is weak where judgement matters. Whether a cost is truly fixed depends on contracts, labour agreements and how quickly management will act, none of which appears in a ledger. A model will label a cost fixed or variable by its name and report a precise break-even built on a wrong split. It also cannot tell whether a price rise will hold or how competitors will respond. The practical rule is to let automation do the arithmetic and the monitoring, and to have a person own the cost split, the assumptions and the decision.

What it means: Case-interview use: for any 'will this make money?' question, split fixed and variable costs, compute break-even and margin of safety, then test which lever (price, volume, variable or fixed cost) is cheapest.
