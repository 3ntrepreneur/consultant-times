SWOT analysis: how to make four boxes useful

Confirmed · 8 min read

Open in the full site →

The short version

A SWOT analysis sorts what is true about a business into four boxes. Strengths and weaknesses are internal: things the company controls or can change, like its brand, costs, people and processes. Opportunities and threats are external: things happening in the market that the company does not control, like customer trends, competitors, regulation and input prices.

The framework is popular because it is simple, and that is also its weakness. A grid of generic bullets (strong brand, rising competition) tells a client nothing they did not know. A SWOT earns its place only when every item is specific, backed by a number, ranked by importance, and connected to a decision.

How to do it properly

Start with a question, not a grid. A SWOT of the whole company is rarely useful; a SWOT for a decision, such as whether to open in a new country or add automation, is. The question decides which facts matter.

Then use one test to sort every item: could the company change it next year? If yes, it is internal (a strength or weakness). If no, it is external (an opportunity or threat). Most mistakes come from putting an outside trend, like rising wages, into the weakness box.

Attach evidence to every bullet: a figure, a date and a source. Then rank each box by two things, how much it could move the results and how likely it is. Keep the top three or four in each box and drop the rest.

A worked example: Chipotle

Chipotle's third-quarter 2026 results give plenty of evidence. Strengths: it owns all of its restaurants in North America, so it keeps the whole margin (23.3% restaurant-level margin in the quarter); digital orders are 38.8% of food and beverage sales; and its loyalty programme accounts for more than a third of sales. Its design is built around throughput, serving more people in the lunch rush.

Weaknesses: that same ownership means no franchisee to absorb cost increases. Margin fell from 24.5% to 23.3% in a year, and labour rose to 25.7% of revenue from 25.2%. The business also had a poor 2025, with comparable sales down 1.7% and customer counts down 3.2%, so it is recovering rather than cruising.

Opportunities: roughly 350 to 370 new restaurants planned for 2026, including 10 to 15 international partner-operated ones; drive-through Chipotlanes in about 80% of new restaurants; and automation such as the Cook to Needs forecasting tool, already in more than 500 restaurants, plus the robotic makeline and avocado machine being tested.

Threats: a food-safety incident would hit the whole brand at once; beef and freight costs push up food costs; further price increases could push customers away; and rivals such as Sweetgreen and Cava are building their own automation.

From four boxes to choices

The step most people skip is turning the grid into strategy. A common method is a TOWS matrix, which crosses the boxes: use strengths to seize opportunities, use strengths to blunt threats, fix weaknesses that block opportunities, and protect against the combination of weaknesses and threats.

For Chipotle: its strong throughput and digital base (strengths) support rapid rollout of Chipotlanes in new stores (opportunity). Its exposure to labour inflation (weakness) is the reason automation (opportunity) matters. Its single-brand risk (threat) argues for a very high food-safety standard. Each crossing becomes a candidate strategy, which you then rank and assign an owner.

The deliverable is not the grid. It is a short list of prioritised moves, each tied to evidence.

Common mistakes

The usual failures are generic bullets with no numbers, mixing internal and external factors, listing 20 items per box, treating every item as equal, and stopping at the list. A related error is using a SWOT as the whole analysis: it organises facts but does not size markets, test economics or compare options. It works best as a summary step after real research.

Another trap is looking only at the company's own point of view. A strength matters only relative to competitors: a loyal customer base is a strength if rivals lack one, and table stakes if they all have it.

Where AI and automation hit

AI tools will draft a SWOT in seconds, and the result is usually fluent but generic. Their useful role is evidence gathering: pulling figures from filings, summarising earnings calls and spotting competitor moves. The judgment, which items matter and what to do about them, is still the consultant's job, and it is where the value is.
