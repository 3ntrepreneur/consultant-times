Porter's Five Forces: judging an industry before you judge a company

Confirmed · 9 min read

Open in the full site →

The short version

Porter's Five Forces is a way to judge how attractive an industry is, before you judge any one company in it. Michael Porter's argument is that profit is not only about how well firms run themselves. It is also about the structure around them. Five pressures decide how much of the value an industry creates is kept by the firms inside it, and how much leaks away to others.

The five forces are: rivalry among existing competitors; the threat of new entrants; the threat of substitutes; the bargaining power of suppliers; and the bargaining power of buyers. Strong forces squeeze profit, weak forces protect it. The output is a verdict on the industry's margin ceiling, which is why the framework sits in the strategy toolkit: it tells you whether the game is worth playing, and which part of the game to play.

The master question for this piece is: why would this business still be winning in ten years? Five Forces answers the first half. It shows what the industry will let any player earn, and which forces a player could change by its own choices.

How to do it properly

Start by drawing the boundary. An industry is a set of firms whose products customers treat as close substitutes, in a defined geography. 'Food' is too wide. 'Supermarkets and supercentres in Canada' is workable, and you may need to split it further, for example into hard discount and full-service, because the forces differ by segment.

Then take each force and ask a specific question, with evidence. Rivalry: how many players are there, how similar are they, and do they compete on price or on something else? Entry: what would a newcomer need (capital, sites, supply chain, brand) and could incumbents block it? Substitutes: what else solves the same need, such as meal kits, restaurants or online delivery? Supplier power: do a few suppliers control something you cannot do without, or can you switch? Buyer power: are customers concentrated or able to switch cheaply?

Rate each force as low, moderate or high, and write the one fact that justifies the rating. This is where most analyses fail: a force is not 'high' because it sounds threatening, it is high because a number or a mechanism shows it. Look for facts like market shares, switching costs, share of costs a supplier represents, and the fixed-cost base that makes rivals fight for volume.

Finally, look at direction and at what is changing. Five Forces is a snapshot, so ask which force is moving, who or what is moving it (technology, regulation, a new entrant) and what that does to the margin ceiling. Many analysts also add a sixth lens, complementors or government, because regulation can reshape a force directly. We will see that in grocery.

A worked example

Our industry is Canadian grocery retail. The Competition Bureau's 2023 market study found that most Canadians buy groceries from one of five companies: Loblaw, Sobeys (owned by Empire), Metro, Costco and Walmart. It reported that Loblaw, Sobeys and Metro each had more than 1,000 stores, including franchised ones, and that Costco and Walmart together had about 500. A 2026 Bureau update repeats that five-company finding.

Rivalry: moderate to high on the surface, but cushioned. Rivals are few, which usually dampens price wars. Yet they fight hard on value. Loblaw said in its second-quarter 2026 release (quarter ended June 20, 2026) that its discount banners again outperformed, and it opened 7 hard discount stores in the quarter. Empire's food same-store sales grew 1.2% in its quarter ended August 1, 2026, and it is rolling out its FreshCo discount banner in Atlantic Canada. Competition has moved to the discount aisle, and to promotions. Loblaw's September 2026 capital plan notes that the share of grocery sales bought on promotion has risen four percentage points since 2022.

New entrants: low to moderate threat. The Bureau pointed to property controls, which are clauses in leases or deeds that stop a rival grocer using a site, as a barrier that blocks new stores. It also noted that capital needs, low population density and the grocers' ownership of many discount banners make standalone entry hard. Policy is now pushing the barrier down: Manitoba passed a law barring new restrictive covenants in grocery, and the Bureau reports Sobeys, Loblaw and Walmart Canada have taken steps to remove such controls. Entry is not impossible, though. The Bureau lists online grocers as an area of innovation, and Retail Insider reports T&T planning new Ontario and Manitoba stores for 2028.

Substitutes: low to moderate. Restaurants, meal kits and delivery compete for some meals, but groceries are a necessity and most spending is hard to substitute. The real substitution happens inside the category, as shoppers trade down. Statistics Canada reports grocery prices rose 1.7% year over year in September 2026, the smallest annual increase since May 2024, but are still up 33.0% since September 2020. That cumulative rise is why shoppers move to discount banners and store brands.

Supplier power: mixed. For large national brands, the big grocers are crucial customers, and the Bureau noted that they receive supplier payments for shelf placement that independents generally do not. But suppliers are often large too. The relationship was serious enough that the industry built a Grocery Code of Conduct, which took full effect on January 1, 2026. It was prompted by supplier fees introduced by Walmart Canada and Loblaw in 2020, according to the Canadian Press. By July 2026 it had received 14 complaints, and 200 businesses had signed on, including all major grocers, though only a few hundred of the few thousand suppliers had. Read this as a sign that supplier power is strong enough to matter, and weak enough in some segments that a code was needed.

Buyer power: low per shopper, but price-aware. A single shopper has no leverage. But shoppers can switch. In the Bureau's 2023 survey, 79% said they visit multiple stores at least sometimes to find the best price, and 75% hold a loyalty card. Loyalty cards raise switching costs. Collectively, buyers also have political power: affordability is why Parliament and regulators keep returning to this sector.

The verdict: the industry structure supports healthy but politically exposed profits. The Bureau reported that the three biggest grocers' combined profit rose from about $2.4 billion in 2019 to $3.6 billion in 2022 (about 50%, computed). Loblaw alone earned $1,994 million of the $3,589 million in 2022 (about 56%, computed from the Bureau's table). Loblaw's retail gross margin in Q2 2026 was 32.2%, and its return on equity was 27.2% according to MarketBeat's summary of the earnings call. Those are strong figures. They are exactly what the forces predict: few rivals, high entry barriers and a necessity product. The caveat is that the Bureau said it could not obtain complete food-only margin data, so net margin conclusions need care.

From analysis to choices

A Five Forces grid is only useful if it leads to choices. The standard move is to ask, for each force, how a firm could weaken it or position itself where it is weakest. Think of it as three plays: position the firm where forces are weakest, shift the forces in its favour, or exploit change before others do.

Take Loblaw. The strongest pressure on margin is buyers moving to value. Its response, from its own releases, is to build the discount position itself: the 2026 plan puts about $2.4 billion into capital spending, with grocery expansion focused mainly on No Frills and Maxi. That keeps price-sensitive shoppers inside its own network, which reduces rivalry's bite. A discount store that you own cannot take sales from you.

Take Empire. The same logic explains rolling FreshCo into Atlantic Canada. It also explains the scale of its fiscal 2027 plan: Retail Insider reports a target of more than 25 new stores and roughly $850 million of capital spending, about half for renovations and store development.

Take a challenger, such as a regional independent or foreign entrant. Five Forces says the barrier is sites and supply, so the choice is where to enter: places where property controls are being removed, formats where scale matters less, or a niche such as ethnic grocery. Take a supplier: the code of conduct is a chance to strengthen its position by joining and documenting practices.

For the policy-maker, the framework points at the weakest link. If entry is the force that most protects profit, then the Bureau's recommendations make sense: ban property controls, back unit pricing so buyers can compare, and support new grocery models. Notice that the Bureau's September 2026 update says progress is partial and urges governments to adopt all four recommendations.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is drawing the industry too wide or too narrow. Analyse 'retail' and every force looks mixed. Analyse one banner and you miss the competition inside the same parent company.

The second is treating the framework as a list of threats. Each force needs a rating and a fact. 'Rivalry is intense' with no evidence is the Five Forces version of a bland SWOT bullet.

The third is forgetting that a force can be high and the industry still profitable. Rivals may be many but disciplined, or the force may fall on someone else in the chain. Always ask who captures the value, and whether firms can pass costs on. Grocery shows this: costs rose, prices rose, and profits held.

The fourth is ignoring dynamics: regulation, technology and a change in consumer habits can move a force within a few years. A static grid in a moving industry is stale.

The fifth is mixing the industry with the firm. Five Forces tells you what the industry will allow. Whether Loblaw earns more than the average depends on its own advantages, such as scale, private labels and loyalty data, which belong in a separate moat analysis. Finally, do not forget numbers: show a market share, a switching cost, or a margin, with its date and source, and label your own arithmetic as computed.

Where AI and automation hit

AI is useful at the evidence stage. It can pull store counts and margins from filings, summarise regulator reports, track price changes and flag when a force shifts, for example when a new discount banner opens in a region. That is hours of research cut to minutes.

AI also changes the forces themselves. Online ordering and delivery lower the barrier for some entrants and raise buyers' ability to compare prices. For incumbents, automation in warehouses and forecasting lowers costs, which is why Empire and Metro are restructuring e-commerce fulfilment, according to Retail Insider. Dynamic pricing and personalised promotions can sharpen rivalry but also shape how the Bureau sees competition.

What AI cannot do is judge. A model will happily call every force 'high' or invent a plausible market share. Check each figure against the source, decide which force is decisive, and write the recommendation. The people who do this well are rated on judgment, not on a pretty grid.

In an interview: Canadian grocery earns healthy profits because a handful of rivals face high entry barriers and sell a necessity, while the main risks are buyers trading down to discount and regulators attacking the barriers.

What it means: Case-interview use: open an 'is this market attractive?' question by naming the five forces, rating each with one fact, and ending with a verdict.
