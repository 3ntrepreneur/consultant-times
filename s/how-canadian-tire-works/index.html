How Canadian Tire works

Confirmed · 12 min read

Open in the full site →

The short version

Canadian Tire looks like a hardware-and-auto shop, but it is better described as three businesses stacked on one customer base. A retail arm sells through stores run by independent dealers and franchisees. A bank, Canadian Tire Bank, lends to those customers on a credit card. A real-estate trust, CT REIT, owns many of the stores and collects rent. A loyalty scheme, Triangle Rewards, ties the three together.

The latest numbers show the scale. In the quarter ended October 3, 2026, the company reported revenue of $4,324.2 million, retail sales of $4,742.8 million and net income attributable to shareholders of $219.6 million, or $4.27 per diluted share, according to its third-quarter release. For 2025, a 53-week year, revenue was $16,315.5 million and normalized diluted EPS was $13.77, up 18.6%, per the fourth-quarter release.

The master question is where the profit actually comes from. The answer is less obvious than the shopfront. In 2025 the Retail segment earned $453.2 million before tax, or $683.7 million on a normalized basis, while Financial Services earned $334.7 million. Add the two reported figures and the bank is about 42% of the total (computed); using the normalized retail figure it is about 33% (computed). If the company stopped selling any single product, little would change. If the credit card or the loyalty scheme stopped working, the company would look quite different.

Run the 60-second test. Who pays? Shoppers at the till, cardholders through interest and fees, and tenants (mostly Canadian Tire itself) through rent. Is revenue recurring? The card balances and rent are; retail sales are repeat but seasonal. Is there a locked-in consumable? Not in the strict sense, but the loyalty money and the card behave like one. Who owns the assets? Mixed: dealers run stores, CT REIT owns property, and the bank carries receivables. What would break it? A credit downturn, a weak consumer, or dealers losing money.

How it makes money

Canadian Tire has at least five engines. The first is selling goods to its network. The annual report says revenue from the sale of goods includes merchandise sold to Dealers, Mark's, SportChek and Party City franchisees, recognised when the goods are delivered, and that a substantial portion of the company's revenue comes from sales to Dealers. In other words, the company is partly a wholesaler to independent operators, not only a shopkeeper. Its corporate website says the Canadian Tire network of about 500 retail operations is managed by third-party operators it calls Dealers.

The second engine is the banner portfolio. At the end of 2025 there were 1,690 retail locations: 673 in the Canadian Tire group (including 171 other-banner stores such as PartSource, PHL and Party City), 354 SportChek, 386 Mark's and 277 gas bars, according to the annual report. Party City in Canada is operated under the Party City name as part of Canadian Tire Retail. Helly Hansen, the outdoor-apparel brand, is no longer part of the story: it was sold to Kontoor Brands on May 31, 2025 for gross proceeds of $1,313.4 million and is shown as discontinued operations.

The third engine is Financial Services. Canadian Tire Bank issues the credit cards and earns interest and fees. The 2025 annual report shows segment revenue of $1,593.8 million, income before tax of $334.7 million, and gross credit-card receivables of $7,555.9 million. The bank funds itself partly from deposits: $2,957.5 million of broker GIC deposits and $584.6 million of retail deposits at year-end. The annual report passages we read did not name a Triangle Mastercard, so we describe it only as the company's credit-card offering.

The fourth is property. CT REIT is majority-owned (67.9% per the Q3 release) and in 2025 reported property revenue of $604.3 million and net operating income of $478.7 million. The company collects its share through distributions, $39.9 million in Q3 2026 alone, recorded in Retail other income. The fifth engine is the loyalty and owned-brand layer, covered below. Stacking these is the point: a franchise-style wholesale margin, a lending margin, rent and a private-label margin all sit on the same footfall.

The economics

Start with retail. Consolidated gross margin in 2025 was $5,615.3 million on revenue of $16,315.5 million, about 34.4% (computed from the annual report). In Q3 2026 the retail gross margin rate excluding petroleum was 35.6%, down 16 basis points, and management's stated long-term anchor is 35% or better. Retail SG&A in the quarter was $784.1 million, up 0.5%, and Retail income before tax was $197.9 million against $144.6 million a year earlier.

Sales per square foot is the trade measure for a store network. SportChek was at $346, up 5.8%, on a rolling 12-month basis in Q3 2026 with 311 stores, down from 319, according to Retail Insider. The pattern is fewer, better stores: SportChek counts fell from 371 to 354 across 2025, while Mark's rose from 383 to 386.

Now the bank. Receivables were $7,555.9 million at the end of 2025. Financial Services income before tax of $334.7 million is about 4.4% of average gross receivables of $7,524 million (computed). That return is large only because the book is high-yielding and risky: the net write-off rate was 7.2% and the allowance rate 12.0% in Q4 2025, according to the annual report. In Q3 2026, income before tax rose to $99.2 million from $84.4 million, with receivables up 4.2% and the allowance unchanged at $935 million, per the earnings call summary on Investing.com. Quarterly Financial Services profit was about 31% of consolidated income before tax of $319.4 million (computed).

Third, the capital. Retail return on invested capital was 11.2% in Q3 2026, up from 10.6%, and the company plans about $2 billion of operating capital investment across 2025 to 2028, with 2026 operating capex of $450 million to $500 million. It spent $442.4 million buying back shares in 2025, and raised the annual dividend to $7.30 from $7.20, its 17th consecutive increase.

A warning on noise: reported 2025 diluted EPS from continuing operations was $10.57 against $14.91, while the normalized figure rose to $13.77 from $11.61. The reported fall reflects items outside the underlying business, so we use normalized figures where the company discloses them and say so.

The number that runs the business

If one number must be chosen, it is the share of retail sales that goes through the loyalty programme, 55.5% on a direct-scan basis in 2025, up 112 basis points, according to the annual report. It runs the business because it connects the three profit pools. A shopper who scans Triangle earns Canadian Tire Money, is marketed to with data, is more likely to carry the card and so generates interest, and shops again in a store sitting on CT REIT land.

Triangle had 9.8 million active registered members at the end of 2025, up 6%. Partners are being added: Petro-Canada and RBC are active, WestJet was due in Q2 2026 and Tim Hortons in the second half. In Q3 2026 loyalty sales grew 6.3% against retail sales growth of 4.4%, and about three million unique members were engaged through partners, up from about two million, though only about 15% were linked to more than one partner, according to Retail Insider and management. Management targets four million over time.

The second number to watch is the credit net write-off rate, 7.2% in both Q4 2025 and Q3 2026, because the bank's profit sits on the gap between interest earned and losses. Tariffs and a weak consumer put pressure on exactly this line. Management said on the call that insolvencies were lower than feared, which is encouraging but is a statement, not a guarantee.

A word on private labels. The 2025 annual report gives Owned Brands penetration of 36.5% of Retail sales, a slight dip from 36.7%. Owned brands matter because the company captures the manufacturer margin as well as the retail one, and because shoppers who learn the labels have fewer reasons to compare elsewhere. The sections we read did not name individual brands, so we do not list them.

Where AI and automation hit

AI at Canadian Tire is mostly about pricing, assortment and marketing, not about replacing labour on the shop floor. The FY2025 release describes DaiVID, an AI pricing and margin tool, and in-stock optimisation as live. On the Q3 2026 call, management said its modules would reach Mark's and SportChek in Q4, mainly for 2027 value, and that the Mosaic customer-data platform guided the back-to-school programme and informs assortment and marketing.

The company is also building a store-operations AI platform with Microsoft and its dealers, according to the call summary. That matters because dealers, not head office, employ the store staff and hold much of the inventory, so any productivity gain must be sold to them as well as installed. In Q3 corporate inventory rose 6% while dealer inventory fell 1%, a reminder that the two balance sheets move separately.

The release says 2026 savings will be balanced against investments including AI deployment, so savings are not simply flowing to profit. Exposure on the other side is real: AI shopping agents that compare prices across retailers weaken a business built on habit and a loyalty scheme. Financial Services can use AI for underwriting too, but the filings we read gave no figures for any AI-driven saving, so we state none.

Who wins and why

The moat is a combination rather than a single wall. Brand and network: more than 1,600 retail and gas outlets and a brand that is almost a household word. Switching costs: Triangle Rewards balances and the card. Scale and cost: owned brands and national buying. Property: CT REIT holds more than 380 properties at 99.5% occupancy, though Canadian Tire is its dominant tenant at 92.0% of gross leasable area and 90.5% of base minimum rent, per the REIT's Q3 release. The REIT is a stable income stream, but it is not independent evidence of demand; it depends on the parent paying rent.

The dealer model is the least visible and most important piece. Dealers are independent operators who contract with the company, buy merchandise from it and finance inventory and fixed assets partly through Franchise Trust, whose average assets were $550.9 million in 2025. Franchise Trust debt is a legal liability of the dealers, so it sits outside the company's capital definition. The company renegotiated its CTR dealer contracts in Q3 2025. Our sources do not give the number of dealers or their compensation formula, so we do not state them. The consultant's point is incentive design: dealers carry local risk, and the head office must keep them profitable enough to keep investing.

The current strategy, called True North, runs from March 2025 for four years. By the end of 2025 it had delivered 52 store projects, a restructuring with roughly $30 million of run-rate savings, and a Hudson's Bay Company brand capsule. Headlines on Q3 2026 show strong comparable sales (3.2% overall, with Automotive on a 25th consecutive quarter of growth at the Canadian Tire banner and SportChek on its ninth), and a headline that the company is putting $200 million behind Mark's new store format, though we did not read the details.

What could break it? First, credit: a sharp rise in unemployment lifts write-offs on a $7.6 billion book. Second, the consumer: management said consumer confidence has dropped sharply and expects value pricing to persist for another 12 to 18 months, and the Q4 started slowly because of warm weather, a reminder of how seasonal the product mix is. Third, margin: tariffs, fuel and transport costs are headwinds into at least the first half of 2027 per management, against a 35% gross-margin anchor. Fourth, dealer health. Fifth, competition from online and mass retailers. On the positive side Q3 2026 net income was $219.6 million against $169.1 million, and retail return on capital is improving.

A business that makes money from selling goods, lending to its own shoppers and renting its own land can survive a weak year in any one of the three, but not a weak year in all three at once, which is what a recession with high unemployment would deliver.

What it means: Canadian Tire is a retailer whose profit is shaped by a bank, a landlord and a loyalty scheme. Retailers, banks, REIT investors and private-equity buyers hire consultants to benchmark loyalty economics, credit-card profitability, dealer or franchise contracts and private-label strategy.
