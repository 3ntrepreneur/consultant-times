How Canadian telecom makes money

Confirmed · 11 min read

Open in the full site →

The short version

A Canadian phone and internet company is a toll collector on a network it had to build first. It pays up front for towers, fibre and radio spectrum, then rents access to households and businesses by the month. The profit comes from how many people pay, how much each pays, and how few leave, set against a bill for equipment that never stops growing.

The three big carriers are large and highly profitable on paper. In the third quarter of 2026, Rogers reported revenue of C$5,443 million and adjusted EBITDA of C$2,535 million, BCE reported C$6,182 million and C$2,786 million, and Telus reported C$5,020 million and C$1,760 million. Added together, that is C$16,645 million of revenue and C$7,081 million of adjusted EBITDA, a combined margin of about 42.5% (computed). The companies define and mix their segments differently, so the total is a rough scale rather than a clean comparison.

The structure is tight. The CRTC's 2026 market report, which covers 2024, puts Canadian telecom revenue at C$59.6 billion, of which mobile was C$33.6 billion, or 56.4%. It says the three largest operators hold about 90% of the national wireless market and account for over 90% of wireless EBITDA.

The quarter's theme was slowing growth. Rogers added only 6,000 postpaid phone customers against 62,000 a year earlier, and its chief executive estimated that industry-wide wireless net additions fell 30 to 40%, according to a trade report on the call. When the market stops adding people quickly, carriers have to win on price, retention and cost, and that is where the money gets made or lost.

How it makes money

There are three engines. The first is wireless: a monthly plan multiplied by the number of subscribers, plus phones sold through the carrier. Rogers' wireless segment earned C$2,033 million of service revenue and C$655 million of equipment revenue in the quarter. Bell's wireless service revenue was C$1,781 million, down 1.3%, and Telus' consolidated service revenue was about C$4.5 billion, down 1%.

The second engine is the fixed network: home internet, TV and phone over cable or fibre. Rogers' cable arm earned C$1,987 million of revenue at a 58.6% margin, with 4,531,000 retail internet subscribers and 10,000 net additions, down from 29,000. Bell reported 3,697,621 residential fibre-to-the-home subscribers, up 2.8%, while its total high-speed internet base fell 2.1% to 4,942,447. Cable video and home phone are shrinking, since Rogers lost about 110,000 video and 114,000 home phone subscribers in a year (computed from 2,414K vs 2,524K and 1,307K vs 1,421K).

The third engine is everything else: media, business services and, increasingly, adjacent bets. Rogers' media arm, which includes Sportsnet, earned C$821 million at a 10.2% margin. Bell Media earned C$803 million, and Bell's business unit reported revenue up 9.2% with AI-powered solutions revenue up 41%. Telus runs a health arm and a digital services arm; the quarter included a C$800 million non-cash impairment on Telus Health, which turned a profit line into a net loss of C$660 million.

The price per customer is the headline unit. Mobile phone ARPU, the average monthly revenue per user, was C$57.22 at Telus (flat), C$57.07 at Bell (down 1.7%) and C$55.22 at Rogers (down 2.6%, from C$56.70). Two of the three cite the CRTC's ban on certain activation and modification fees as part of the fall. Rogers' finance chief said customers added in Q3 and October carry ARPU about 8% higher than a year ago, which suggests the pricing floor may be stabilising, though that is management's claim.

The economics

Start with cost structure. BCE's operating costs were C$3,396 million on C$6,182 million of revenue, about 54.9% of revenue (computed), which leaves its 45.1% EBITDA margin. Rogers' is 46.6%; Telus' is about 35.1% (computed), lower because it carries the thinner-margin digital services business, whose adjusted EBITDA was C$62 million on C$637 million of revenue, roughly 9.7% (computed). BCE's release does not give a labour-versus-other split in the text I read, so I will not guess one. The signal on labour is in the cuts: Rogers booked C$104 million of restructuring, mostly severance, in the quarter, and BCE said Bell Canada's costs fell because of workforce reductions.

The margin is high because the marginal customer costs little once the network exists. Rogers' wireless segment earned a 68.0% margin on service revenue (up from 66.7%) even as service revenue fell 1%. Wireless produced C$1,383 million of Rogers' C$2,535 million consolidated EBITDA, about 55% (computed), so the wireless profit pool dominates, followed by cable, with media a small contributor.

What eats the cash is capital spending. Bell spent C$1,330 million in the quarter, 21.5% of revenue, up 49.3%, with its AI data centre build-out and its U.S. fibre business as stated drivers. Telus spent C$701 million, about 14.0% of revenue (computed), and guides to roughly C$2.6 billion for the year. Rogers spent C$524 million, down 46%, about 9.6% of revenue (computed); the release text I read did not explain the timing. Together the three spent C$2,555 million, about 36% of their combined EBITDA (computed).

Debt makes the margin a smaller prize. Rogers' leverage ratio was 3.7 times at quarter-end, down from 4.0 at December 2025, and Telus' net debt was 3.6 times EBITDA against a target of 3.0 or lower by the end of 2028. Rogers' free cash flow rose 65% to C$1,370 million, partly because capex fell, and it then paid C$4.35 billion in cash on October 1 for the remaining stake in MLSE, funded from credit facilities.

The cycle behaviour is defensive. People keep their phone and internet in a downturn, and the CRTC counts telecom revenue as flat in 2024. The swing factors are net additions, promotions and regulation, not the business cycle.

The number that runs the business

The number is the product of three figures: subscribers, ARPU and churn. Churn is the share of subscribers who leave each month. Postpaid churn was 1.01% at Telus (a three-year low), 1.03% at Rogers (up from 0.99%) and 1.06% at Bell (down 7 basis points). A monthly rate near 1% sounds trivial, but compounded over twelve months it means roughly one subscriber in nine leaves each year (computed), and each one has to be replaced by marketing, discounts and handset subsidies before the base grows at all.

This is why a carrier will accept a lower ARPU to keep a customer. Prepaid is the extreme: churn was 4.77% at Rogers (from 2.86%) and 5.32% at Bell (from 5.10%), which makes prepaid a high-turnover, low-loyalty product.

Here is the counter-intuitive fact. Telus won the quarter on subscribers, with 84,000 net phone additions against Bell's 24,000 postpaid and Rogers' 6,000, yet its adjusted EBITDA fell 5%. Rogers added the fewest customers and its EBITDA rose 1%, because a bigger share of each service dollar was kept. In this industry, who grows the fastest is not the same as who earns the most; cost control and ARPU decide it. Trade press adds that Quebecor, which owns Freedom, Fizz and Videotron, added nearly 137,000 lines, the fastest of any carrier.

ARPU is also where the regulator touches earnings most directly. The CRTC report puts 2024 mobile ARPU at about C$68.41 including devices and about C$51 excluding them, both lower than in 2023. Falling ARPU is a multi-year trend, not a one-quarter event.

Where AI and automation hit

AI shows up in these filings as a growth bet first and a cost saver second. Bell's AI Fabric data centre programme is named as a driver of its capex jump, and BCE announced a non-binding memorandum for up to 900 MW of further capacity in Saskatchewan. Bell's AI-powered solutions revenue grew 41%. Telus says it intends to become a leading communications and AI infrastructure player in Canada.

On costs, the evidence is thinner. BCE credits technology and automation-enabled operating efficiencies for part of the cost reduction at Bell Canada but also says AI-powered solutions growth raises costs, and it gives no figure for AI savings. Severance was C$11 million in the quarter and C$59 million year to date, against C$250 million over the same nine months of 2025. Telus targets about C$900 million of annualised cash savings exiting 2028, around 80% from core telecom, and raised its 2026 restructuring cost assumption to about C$900 million from about C$500 million. The release does not say how much of this is AI.

My read, as analysis: AI helps most in customer care, network operations and billing, which are large cost lines, but its effect is invisible in revenue per user. The bigger strategic question is whether AI traffic and data centres become a new profit pool for network owners, or just another capex programme with uncertain returns. Bell's capex intensity of 21.5% is the early cost of that bet.

Who wins and why

The regulator sets the edges. The CRTC's Policy 2026-43, issued March 12, 2026, prohibits certain activation and modification fees, and on June 30 it asked Rogers, Bell and Telus to show cause on other fees. For wholesale, a decision finalised rates for some high-speed internet services and required an aggregated wholesale fibre service, and Bell's release says a retroactive effect hurt its Q2 wireline revenue. For wireless, regional carriers with their own spectrum can use the big networks under a seven-year mandated MVNO access arrangement set in Policy 2021-130, with rates negotiated or settled by final-offer arbitration. The CRTC says new competitors took over 55% of mobile net additions in 2024, which shows the wholesale route is being used.

Spectrum is the other gate. Canada's 3500 MHz auction in 2021 raised C$8.91 billion in total. The next mmWave auction was pushed from October 19, 2027 to January 25, 2028, according to Rogers' release, and Telus paid more than C$53 million in spectrum renewal fees to ISED in the first nine months of 2026. Spectrum is a licence, so it is a durable moat, but it is also a recurring bill.

For British Columbia, Telus is the local reference point: its releases are datelined Vancouver, and its C$900 million efficiency plan, C$2.6 billion capex guide and C$0.1875 quarterly dividend all matter to its investors and to anyone who works with a Vancouver-headquartered carrier. The release does not give a B.C. split or say where jobs will fall, so I will not guess.

So who wins? The carrier with the lowest churn and the lowest cost per connection, which can keep ARPU from falling while it pays down debt. Telus has the best churn this quarter but the weakest earnings. Rogers has the healthiest margin and cash flow but is adding the fewest subscribers and now carries a bigger sports bill. Bell is spending the most for a future in fibre and AI data centres. None of the three has solved slower growth, and the CRTC is still pushing prices lower.

What it means: Carriers face falling revenue per user, fee rules under CRTC review and a fresh capex cycle in fibre and AI infrastructure, so they need cost, pricing and churn analysis. Investors and B.C. employers following Telus will want scenarios on its efficiency plan and leverage target.
