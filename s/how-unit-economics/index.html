Unit economics: knowing what one customer is worth

Confirmed · 12 min read

Open in the full site →

The short version

Unit economics asks a plain question: for one customer, how much money comes in, how much it costs to win and serve them, and how long until the account pays for itself. A business can grow fast and still lose money on every customer. A business can also look small and be very healthy if each customer is worth far more than it cost to find. The framework turns that into a handful of numbers a team can track and argue about.

The terms are short. CAC (customer acquisition cost) is the sales and marketing spend needed to win one new customer. Churn is the share of customers who leave in a period. LTV (lifetime value) is the total revenue a customer is expected to bring before leaving. Gross-margin-adjusted LTV counts only the gross profit from that revenue, after the direct cost of serving the customer. Payback period is the time it takes for a customer's gross profit to repay the CAC. LTV:CAC is lifetime value divided by acquisition cost. Contribution margin is what is left of revenue after all variable costs, including those of serving, collecting and supporting the customer, but before fixed overheads.

The master question for this piece is: why would this business still be winning in ten years? Unit economics answers a narrow part of it. It shows whether each new customer adds to the firm's value or drains it, and which lever (price, retention, cost to serve or cost to win) matters most. It does not show whether customers will still want the product in ten years, which is a question about moat and demand.

How to do it properly

Start with the customer definition. A customer must mean the same thing in the numerator and the denominator. Count paying customers, not sign-ups, unless free users are part of the funnel you are pricing. Split the analysis by segment, channel or cohort (customers who joined in the same month), because an average across very different groups hides the answer. A cheap channel that brings in short-lived customers can look good on CAC and bad on LTV.

Next, measure CAC fully. Include the salaries of the sales and marketing team, advertising, tools and agency fees, not only media spend. Use spend from the period before the customers arrived if sales cycles are long. Separate paid CAC from blended CAC: blended CAC divides all spend by all new customers, including those who came through word of mouth, so it flatters the paid channels. Most public companies report only total sales and marketing expense, which means an outsider can only compute rough proxies, not the company's own CAC.

Then estimate LTV. The standard shortcut is monthly revenue per customer, multiplied by gross margin, divided by monthly churn. The logic is that expected lifetime in months is about one divided by the monthly churn rate. Two cautions follow. First, use gross margin, not revenue, otherwise a low-margin business looks richer than it is. Second, churn is an estimate that can move a lot: an essay on LTV by Jason Cohen (A Smart Bear) argues LTV is not a stable, knowable number and can change by roughly 2x within a year, and that it should not be used alone to set acquisition spending.

Finally, compute payback: CAC divided by monthly gross profit per customer. Payback is more robust than LTV because it needs no forecast beyond the first months, and it speaks directly to cash. A firm with long payback has to fund growth with capital; a firm with short payback can recycle cash into acquisition. Contribution margin belongs here too: if support, payment fees or delivery costs vary with each customer, subtract them, otherwise payback is understated.

Benchmarks help you ask whether your numbers look odd, but they are not goals. David Skok, a venture investor, wrote that a guideline for a successful software-as-a-service business is that LTV:CAC should be higher than 3, and that this assumes the simpler LTV formula and a gross margin of 80% or higher (typical guideline, published by a VC, not a rule). He also notes that his under-12-months guideline for recovering CAC was written in 2011 and is dated, and that enterprise land-and-expand models can run around 20 months. Cohen's essay reports the common view that 3 is healthy and 5 very good, but questions the approach. The ratio of 3 is a rule of thumb for a certain kind of subscription software with high gross margin. It does not tell a consumer app, a marketplace or a hardware seller what to target.

A worked example

First, a small illustration. Every number in this block is an ASSUMPTION for teaching, not data from any company. Suppose a subscription costs $10 a month, and serving it costs $2 a month, so gross margin is 80% (assumption). Suppose card fees and support cost another $1 a month per customer, so contribution margin is $7 a month, or 70% of revenue (assumption). Suppose monthly churn is 5%, so expected lifetime is 1 / 0.05 = 20 months (computed from the assumption). Simple LTV is $10 x 20 = $200. Gross-margin-adjusted LTV is $8 x 20 = $160. If CAC is $50 (assumption), LTV:CAC on the adjusted figure is 160 / 50 = 3.2, and payback is $50 / $8 = about 6 months (computed). Change churn to 8% and lifetime falls to 12.5 months, adjusted LTV to $100, and the ratio to 2.0, with no change in marketing at all. That sensitivity is the point of the exercise.

Now a real company: Duolingo, the language-learning app. It is a useful case because it reports paid subscribers, daily users, gross margin and sales and marketing expense every quarter, and because most of its users are free. Its figures come from its third-quarter 2026 shareholder letter, filed with the SEC. In that quarter it reported total revenue of $309.0 million, of which $270.0 million was subscription revenue, against $271.7 million and $229.5 million a year earlier. Paid subscribers at the end of the quarter were 13.1 million, up from 11.5 million. Daily active users were 64.9 million and monthly active users 153.1 million. Gross profit was $225.1 million, a gross margin of 72.9%, up from 72.5%. GAAP sales and marketing expense was $42.2 million, against $35.1 million a year earlier.

What can an outsider compute? Sales and marketing was about 13.6% of revenue (computed: 42.2 / 309.0). Paid subscribers were about 8.6% of monthly active users (computed: 13.1 / 153.1), which shows the funnel: most of the 153.1 million users pay nothing, and the free tier is both a product and an acquisition channel. Subscription revenue per period-end paid subscriber was about $20.6 for the quarter, or about $6.9 a month (computed: 270.0 / 13.1, divided by three; this ignores that subscribers changed during the quarter, and that subscription revenue includes some plan types with different prices). Applying the company-wide gross margin of 72.9% gives about $5.0 of gross profit per subscriber per month (computed; the margin is for the whole company, not only subscriptions).

Duolingo does not disclose CAC, subscriber churn or retention in the letter, so a true LTV:CAC cannot be computed from public data. We can, however, run a deliberately harsh test. Net paid subscriber additions over the year were 1.6 million (computed: 13.1 - 11.5). If we assume the third-quarter sales and marketing figure repeats in each of four quarters, annual spend is about $169 million (est., computed: 4 x 42.2), and dividing by net additions gives about $105 per net new subscriber (computed). At $5.0 of monthly gross profit, that implies payback of about 21 months (computed). This is an upper bound and not Duolingo's CAC: net additions are lower than gross additions because some subscribers leave, the spending also supports free users and retention of existing users, and all of it is annualised from one quarter. The real figure is likely lower.

The same filing contains a clue about where the company is pointing the lever. Daily users rose to 64.9 million from 50.5 million, about 29% (computed), faster than paid subscribers, which rose about 14% (computed). Duolingo says it ran experiments aimed at increasing user retention and DAUs. The reading, an inference and not a company statement, is that growing free daily use feeds the paid funnel more cheaply than buying new users.

From analysis to choices

A set of unit-economics numbers is only useful if it changes a decision. There are four levers: raise price or revenue per customer, cut the cost to serve, lower CAC, or lengthen customer life by reducing churn. Test each against the others. A 1-point cut in monthly churn often beats a 10% cut in CAC, because it lifts LTV for every customer already won, while a CAC cut helps only future ones. The worked illustration above shows this: moving churn from 5% to 8% cut the ratio from 3.2 to 2.0.

Next, decide how fast to spend. If payback is short and the cohort is reliable, spending more on acquisition grows value, until the marginal customer (the next one, from a costlier channel) pays back too slowly. Average CAC is not marginal CAC. A company should compare channels at the margin and stop where payback exceeds what it can fund or how long it expects customers to stay.

For Duolingo, the choice visible in the data is the free-to-paid funnel. With paid subscribers at roughly 8.6% of monthly users (computed), a small improvement in conversion adds many paying customers at almost no extra acquisition cost, and daily habit is the likely driver of that conversion. That is an inference from the company's stated focus on retention and DAUs, not a company statement about its own conversion economics. The choice for management is how much to push monetisation (which can annoy free users) against engagement (which feeds future conversion).

For an investor or consultant, the next step is to ask for what is missing: subscriber churn by cohort, gross additions, and split of S&M between acquiring users and retaining them. Without them, any LTV:CAC figure for a public company is an estimate, and should be labelled as one. A recommendation built on a benchmark alone, such as 'raise spending until the ratio falls to 3', mixes up a rule of thumb with a decision.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is using revenue instead of gross profit. A customer who brings $200 of revenue and $100 of cost is not worth $200. The second is using blended CAC, which includes free word-of-mouth growth, to judge a paid channel; the marginal customer costs more than the average one.

The third is relying on a single churn number. Churn is rarely flat: new customers often leave faster than old ones, and a monthly figure taken from a growing business mixes young and old cohorts. Cohort curves are more honest. The fourth is treating LTV as a precise forecast. As noted above, it can move by a large factor in a year, which makes LTV:CAC a rough signal.

The fifth is applying a benchmark as a target. A ratio of 3 or a payback of 12 months came from software businesses with high margins and, in Skok's own account, the payback guideline dates from 2011. A firm with strong cash, long customer life and lower margin may rationally accept a different number, and one with weak cash may need much shorter payback than any guideline says.

The sixth is leaving out costs and time. Contribution margin should include payment fees, support and discounts, and cash timing matters: annual plans paid up front change payback. Finally, do not mix labels. When a public company does not give CAC, say so, label your proxy as computed and say what it overstates or understates.

Where AI and automation hit

AI helps most with the measurement work. It can pull subscriber counts, margins and marketing expense from filings into a consistent table, rebuild cohorts from raw event data, and flag when a segment's churn drifts. It can also run sensitivity tables in seconds, which makes the point about churn and price easy to see.

AI also changes the inputs. In consumer apps, generative features can raise the cost to serve, because each active user triggers computing costs, which pushes gross margin down; the Duolingo letter shows gross margin at 72.9%, so it is worth watching whether that holds as usage rises. Automation can lower CAC through better targeting and lifecycle messaging, and lower support costs. But if every competitor uses the same tools, the gain in CAC is competed away, so the lasting advantage lies in retention and product, not in the ad machine.

What AI cannot do is decide which number to trust. It will fill a gap with a plausible churn rate or a neat ratio, so every figure must be checked against a filing or labelled as an assumption. It also cannot judge whether spending more on growth is wise given a company's cash and strategy. That is a human decision about risk.

In an interview: unit economics compares what one customer is worth, in gross profit over their life, with what it costs to win them, and a business is healthy when payback is quick and retention is strong, whatever any rule-of-thumb ratio says.

What it means: Case-interview use: for any customer-growth question, compute gross-profit LTV, CAC and payback, then check churn by cohort before judging growth spend.
