How North American freight rail makes money

Confirmed · 11 min read

Open in the full site →

The short version

A freight railroad is a toll road that also owns the trucks. It builds and maintains its own track, signals and yards, which cost roughly the same whether ten trains run or twelve, and then sells space on that network to shippers of grain, chemicals, coal, cars and containers. The business is therefore about two things: how much more it can charge each year, and how many extra cars it can push through track it has already paid for.

Union Pacific shows what that looks like when it works. In the third quarter of 2026 it reported revenue of US$7,197 million and operating income of US$2,943 million, according to its earnings release filed with the SEC. That is an operating margin of about 41% (computed), and an operating ratio, the share of revenue eaten by operating costs, of 59.1%. Few industrial companies earn that on a business that moves coal and corn.

The industry is also tightly held. The Association of American Railroads lists six Class I railroads in North America's freight network and says they take 93% of industry revenue, with about 635 short lines sharing the rest. Union Pacific, Norfolk Southern, BNSF and CSX are among them, as are CN and CPKC in Canada. That concentration is the backdrop to the proposed deal for Union Pacific to buy Norfolk Southern, which trade press reports at an enterprise value of US$85 billion and on which objections, protests and requests for conditions are due at the Surface Transportation Board on November 18, 2026, according to the STB's August 18 decision.

The rest of this piece explains where the margin comes from, why it moves the way it does, and why a rail merger is as much a story about price as about service.

How it makes money

Railroads sell a ton of freight moved over distance. The two headline unit measures are revenue per carload and revenue per revenue ton-mile, which is one ton carried one mile. CN reported freight revenue per revenue ton-mile of 7.70 cents in the third quarter of 2026, up 10%, and freight revenue per carload of C$3,353, up 15%, in its release filed with the SEC.

Revenue is grouped by what is shipped, and the groups behave very differently. Union Pacific splits its book into bulk (grain, coal, fertiliser), industrial (chemicals, metals, construction materials, forest products) and premium (intermodal containers and automobiles). In Q3 2026 premium handled 1,158,000 carloads, about half of the 2,293,000 total (computed), but earned US$1,889 per car, against US$4,004 for bulk and US$4,136 for industrial. Premium therefore made about 32% of freight revenue (computed from US$2,188 million of US$6,813 million) on half the cars.

That mix matters for two reasons. The revenue-per-car gap shows that the cheap-per-car traffic is the high-volume traffic. As analysis rather than a reported figure, intermodal competes with trucks on price, while heavy bulk freight has fewer alternatives over long distances.

The Canadian railroads sell a different basket. CPKC's third-quarter freight revenue was C$4,139 million, of which grain was C$904 million, up 29%, and intermodal C$815 million, up 22%, according to its Q3 release. CN's mix is heavier in petroleum and chemicals (C$1,000 million) and intermodal (C$1,065 million), with grain and fertilisers at C$923 million. Canadian rail leans on export bulk: grain, potash, coal and forest products moving to ports on both coasts.

Union Pacific's 2025 release quotes freight revenue excluding fuel surcharge, which rose 3% in 2025 on a 1% rise in revenue carloads, so price and mix did most of the work in that year.

The economics

Start with what a railroad spends. Union Pacific's Q3 2026 operating expenses were US$4,254 million. By line, compensation and benefits were US$1,264 million (about 30%, computed), fuel US$1,040 million (about 24%), purchased services and materials US$746 million (about 18%), depreciation US$643 million (about 15%), equipment rents US$206 million and other US$355 million, per the release. A year earlier fuel was only US$616 million, about 17% of costs (computed), so fuel is the one big line that swings with the oil market.

Most of the rest is fixed in the short run. Depreciation is the accounting shadow of a network that has to be rebuilt continuously: the AAR says US freight railroads spent about 17% of revenue on capital investment over 2016 to 2025, against around 3% historically for the average US manufacturer. CN planned a 2026 capital programme of about C$2.8 billion, and CPKC spent C$613 million on property additions in the third quarter alone. Crews are semi-fixed too, because a railroad cannot lay off and rehire locomotive engineers by the quarter.

Fixed cost explains the pattern of profit growth. When volume rises on a network that was not full, almost every extra dollar of revenue falls to the bottom line. Union Pacific's Q3 revenue rose US$953 million from a year earlier and operating income rose US$394 million, an incremental margin of about 41% (computed), even with a US$424 million jump in the fuel bill. That is why firm pricing and rising volume can lift profit faster than revenue.

Pricing is the other lever. Union Pacific's average revenue per car rose 8% to US$2,971 in the quarter while carloads rose 6%, so about half of the 15% freight revenue gain came from yield (computed from the release's figures). A railroad that raises rates above its own unit costs every year compounds the margin; one that cannot, because trucks or a rival railroad cap the rate, does not.

That ability is why regulators, and the filings now due at the STB, focus less on whether a merged railroad would run trains well and more on where shippers lose a competitive option. If a shipper can reach two railroads, the rate is disciplined. If it can reach one, the railroad has pricing power.

The number that runs the business

The operating ratio is operating expenses divided by revenue. Lower is better. A 59.1% ratio means 59.1 cents of every revenue dollar goes on running the railroad and 40.9 cents is operating profit (computed). Railroads report it every quarter, and it is the headline measure in each release read for this piece.

The ratio is a blunt tool, and the third quarter shows why. Union Pacific's reported ratio of 59.1% was 0.1 points better than a year earlier, but its adjusted ratio of 58.8% was 0.3 points worse. Management said higher fuel prices cost about 140 basis points of ratio; without fuel, the improvement would have been large. CPKC's reported ratio worsened 130 basis points to 64.8% and its core adjusted ratio worsened 120 basis points to 61.9%, with fuel responsible for 170 and 190 basis points respectively. CN's adjusted ratio improved 40 basis points to 61.0%, while its reported ratio worsened 230 basis points to 63.7% after the C$118 million IT impairment.

Because depreciation sits inside operating costs, a railroad that underinvests in track gets a better ratio today and a worse railroad tomorrow. And because the ratio is a percentage, a railroad can improve it by shedding low-margin freight that still contributes to fixed costs. That is a risk to watch rather than a reported finding.

Here is the counter-intuitive fact. In 2020, when the pandemic cut Union Pacific's carloads by 7% to 7,753,000 and revenue by 10% to US$19,533 million, the reported operating ratio improved, to 59.9% from 60.6% a year earlier, according to the company's fourth-quarter 2020 release. Lower fuel costs, down 38%, and cost cuts more than offset the fixed-cost penalty of lost volume. A downturn does not always show up in the ratio, so the number has to be read with the cycle and the fuel bill beside it.

Operating income still fell. It dropped US$720 million on a US$2,175 million fall in revenue, a decremental margin of about 33% (computed), lower than the 41% margin on the way up in Q3 2026, though the two periods differ in fuel and other conditions.

Where AI and automation hit

The biggest costs in the table above are labour, fuel and the network itself, and automation can reach each only partly. Better scheduling and train planning can raise asset use and reduce fuel burn. Wayside detectors and machine vision can turn inspections that used to be periodic into continuous monitoring, which matters in a business where a bearing failure stops a main line. Back-office work such as billing, customer service and crew management is the easiest to automate.

The sources read for this piece do not quantify any AI saving, so it would be a guess to put a number on one. What the filings do show is the cost of getting technology wrong: CN's C$118 million impairment came from discontinuing certain components of an IT project, and Union Pacific reported US$22 million of acquisition-related expense in the third quarter, a reminder that integration, not software, is the harder project in a rail merger.

The more durable effect may be on pricing and service data. Shippers who see real-time shipment status can compare railroads more easily, which helps the competitive end of the market, intermodal, and does little for captive bulk shippers. That is analysis, not a reported finding, and the next quarter's yield figures will show whether it is true.

Who wins and why

The winners in this industry are railroads with dense networks, balanced traffic and the discipline to price above cost. Union Pacific's 59.1% ratio and 41% incremental margin are the benchmark in this group. The merger would join Union Pacific and Norfolk Southern into a single route of more than 50,000 route miles across 43 states, announced on July 29, 2025. Railpace reported terms of US$320 a share and an enterprise value of US$85 billion; these are trade-press figures, not read from the merger filings. Norfolk Southern's own ratio was not in the sources read.

The opposition is the part of the story due on November 18. According to Trucking & Transport News, CSX, BNSF, CPKC and Grand Trunk Railroad told the STB in early January 2026 that the application was incomplete and should be rejected. The STB accepted a revised application on May 28, 2026, paused it for more information, and in an August 18 decision restarted the clock without ruling on the merits. After November 18, applicants have until February 16, 2027 to respond, and a public hearing date is still to be set. Union Pacific's own target for the review runs into early 2027.

The Canadian angle: CPKC is among the objectors, and CN's release shows it is spending on the review, though it does not state a position, with C$14 million of advisory costs in Q3 and C$48 million year to date that it excludes from adjusted results.

For shippers and for the other railroads, the question is the same: does a combined railroad have more reason to keep a gateway open, or to keep traffic on its own line, where it earns the whole haul? That choice, repeated across thousands of origin-destination pairs, determines the toll. The evidence due on November 18 is mainly about it.

For anyone assessing the sector, the practical test is to look past the headline ratio. Read it ex-fuel, check price against volume, look at incremental margin by commodity group, and look at capital spending against revenue. A railroad that passes all four is earning its margin; one that passes only the first may be running down its network.

What it means: Shippers, short lines and rival railroads are preparing evidence for the November 18 filings, and every one of them needs economic and network analysis. Investors and boards in trucking, ports and bulk commodities will want scenario work on rates, service and interchange if a transcontinental railroad is approved.
