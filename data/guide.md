# Editorial method guide (from the owner's own business-acumen notes)

This guide gives the daily runs the owner's way of thinking about business. It is method only. The notes contained rough
"industry-typical" benchmark figures; those are NOT published here and must never appear on the site as facts. Use them
only to know what to look up, then cite a real source for every number.

## One master question per deep dive
- Business: where does the profit actually come from? If the obvious product stopped selling, would money still be made?
- Industry: what does the industry's structure say about its margin ceiling, regardless of how hard firms try?
- Strategy: why would this business still be winning in ten years?

## The 60-second business-model test (use in every Business piece)
1. Who pays, and for what (user, advertiser, merchant paying a take rate)?
2. Is revenue one-off or recurring, and what is churn?
3. Is there a locked-in consumable (the razorblade)?
4. Who owns the assets (asset-light brand/network versus asset-heavy capital and cycle risk)?
5. What would break it?
Then name every revenue engine. Strong companies stack models (hardware plus platform fees plus subscriptions; franchise royalties plus
real estate plus supply chain). Naming all of them is the mark of having read the filings.

## Model catalogue to recognise
razor-razorblade, subscription, marketplace (take rate), franchise, freemium (funnel, not a model alone), platform/ecosystem,
advertising, float (insurance premiums, deposits, prepaid balances), licensing, agency/hours, asset-light, bundling, loss leader.

## Moat checklist (Strategy and Business pieces)
network effects, switching costs, scale, brand, cost advantage, regulation or licences, intangibles/IP. "Hard work" is not a moat.
Also check disruption (low-end entrant climbs upmarket), price-war dynamics between a few rivals (how do they avoid it?),
vertical integration versus outsourcing (integrate where differentiation lives, outsource commodity), first mover versus fast follower.

## Every Industry piece includes
- a sourced cost structure (what share goes to labour, fuel, rent, COGS, R&D) and the margin it leaves
- where profit pools sit in the value chain and which way they are moving
- concentration and who the players are (verify any claim such as "three firms hold most of the market")
- cycle behaviour: what booms or busts it, which segments are counter-cyclical
- one "counter-intuitive fact" that explains the business (for example where a restaurant chain or airline really earns its profit)

## Pricing, deals and macro pieces
- Pricing: anchoring, decoys, loss aversion, bundling, dynamic pricing, always tied to a real company example.
- Deal math: EV versus equity value, multiples by sector, synergies, unit economics, working capital.
- Macro: show the indicator, what it moves in the sector, and the date of the data; never use stale rates or prices.

## Number discipline (the owner's room rule, adopted by the site)
Every number is one of: a fact from a cited source, a benchmark from a cited published source labelled as typical, or an assumption
labelled as an assumption. A benchmark supports a claim; it never is the recommendation.

## Voice
End each deep dive with one plain sentence a student could say in a case interview ("This is a razorblade business: ...").
